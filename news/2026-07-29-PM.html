The US struck Iran, and half the Patriot stockpile is already gone

CSIS puts interceptors under 1,000 from 2,330 with a three-year rebuild, a drone hit a US-owned LNG ship at Egypt's Damietta port, and Anthony Fauci took the Fifth rather than answer Rand Paul.

WorldPoliticsMarkets

Six months in, the interesting number stopped being casualties and became inventory. Ranked, starting there.

1. The US struck Iran today, and the magazine depth is now the story. Per the Financial Markets list, a US official confirmed air strikes inside Iran with blasts reported in the southwest, framed by the military as a "powerful response to yesterday's attempted Iranian attacks on U.S. forces based in the Middle East." Trump had said it plainly beforehand. "The Iranians know we are going to attack them today, it is our turn. We will hit them hard."

Yesterday's attempt is what he means. The Revolutionary Guard launched multiple ballistic missiles at 5:45pm Eastern at a US base in Jordan, and per the Market list Central Command says every one was intercepted. Overnight, joint US and Saudi strikes on pro-Iranian militias in Iraq killed around twenty Iranian advisers, per an American source to the New York Times, with the sites reportedly chosen because those advisers were there. Saudi Arabia confirmed its participation under Article 51, retaliation for drones that hit petroleum facilities in the Eastern Province and around Riyadh.

Now the part getting almost no attention. Per the Polymarket list, CSIS estimates more than half of American Patriot and THAAD stockpiles have been consumed in this war. Patriot interceptors are under 1,000, down from 2,330. THAAD sits near 250 from 360. More than 1,000 Tomahawks and 1,100 JASSMs have been fired, and rebuilding takes three years or more.

Read what that constrains. Every interception announced as a success spends a round that cannot be replaced this presidential term, and the same stockpile is the one Washington draws on for Ukraine and for Gulf allies. A defence that works perfectly and cannot be resupplied is a clock, and nobody in the daily briefings is reading it out.

2. Somebody hit an LNG ship in Egypt, and the target selection makes no sense on the obvious theory. Per the same list, a US-owned, Marshall-Islands-flagged floating storage facility at Egypt's Damietta port was struck by at least one drone. Anas Alhajji names the vessel as the Gaslog Salem, 155,000 cubic metres, chartered by EGAS for summer storage and arrived only Wednesday midday. He says one liquefaction plant and two floating regasification ships were hit, and then asks the question worth carrying: who benefits from destroying two regasification ships whose job is moving LNG toward Europe?

That is a real puzzle rather than a rhetorical one. Damietta is not a chokepoint and Egypt is not a combatant. Somebody spent a drone on European energy logistics. One source carries the vessel detail, so hold the specifics loosely. The Saudis are separately calling for a coalition of the willing to protect Red Sea shipping from the Houthis.

3. Fauci took the Fifth, and the hearing everyone scheduled turned into a contempt fight. Anthony Fauci was due before Rand Paul today, and per the Kalshi list he invoked the Fifth Amendment and declined to testify at the Senate covid hearing. Paul says the committee will now vote on contempt.

Two days ago this was a calendar item. It is now a legal one. Per the Polymarket AM list, odds on a Fauci arrest nearly doubled after the release of his private diary, sitting near 10 percent. Trump added to it by claiming Biden "probably didn't" sign Fauci's pardon and suggesting an autopen produced it. Established, that would reopen everything the pardon closed. No evidence for it has been produced.

4. The third tariff regime is live, and the economist case against it is that it gave away the leverage. Per Prof G Markets, a new set of tariffs took effect Friday covering 80 countries and more than 99 percent of US imports, built on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 with a stated rationale of countering business lost to forced labour. Countries with a forced-labour ban pay 10 percent, those without pay 12. The average US tariff rate is now 11.1 percent and the Yale Budget Lab expects 11.8 by the end of the year.

These replace the February levies, themselves imposed the same day the Supreme Court struck down the original Liberation Day tariffs, and which expired last week. Justin Wolfers calls this episode three and the dumbest of the three, on the grounds that a flat 10 to 12 percent gives the President nothing to trade with Xi, and he names Jamieson Greer as the architect. His better point is the macro one: both the tariffs and the war are supply shocks, and no central bank can fix a supply shock. Inflation at 3.5 percent against a 2 percent target is the residue.

5. The CLARITY Act finally has a date, a vote count and a hostage. Per Lark Davis, Senate Majority Leader John Thune said on Fox News that the bill gets a floor vote before the August recess, with Russia sanctions ahead of it, and Bloomberg reports nearly ten Democrats want it passed. Hold that against Thune on July 23, when he said there was no runway. Either he moved or the confirmation is softer than it sounds. Per the People to follow list, Charles Schwab puts the deadline at August 10, when the Senate leaves.

Yesterday the blocker was one enforcement clause, over whether state attorneys general or Todd Blanche's Justice Department polices the ethics provisions. Tonight there is a proposed mechanism. Ruben Gallego and Thom Tillis are finalising new ethics language for the White House within days, and Mike Novogratz predicts August passage through a compromise modelled on the Laken Riley Act, letting state attorneys general sue the Justice Department but not the President directly. Brian Armstrong testified that the bill is "at the one-yard line." Elizabeth Warren says it will "blow up the economy." Prediction markets have it at 40 to 42 percent, so the lobbying blitz moved almost nothing.

One footnote deserves more than footnote treatment. Per Jeff Park, the last time this fight was settled was the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000, and the provision that mattered was not the headline. It was the Enron loophole exempting over-the-counter derivatives from oversight, which he calls the single biggest proximate legal cause of 2008. His prediction is that the consequential piece of CLARITY will again read as a footnote and again involve offshore derivatives. That is the clause to find when the text lands.

6. Around the world, quickly.

  • A 7.1-magnitude earthquake hit Japan's Kumamoto prefecture, with 300,000 ordered to evacuate and people reported trapped in a collapsed shopping mall.
  • Boston Dynamics' interim chief executive says Chinese humanoid robots should not be allowed into the United States under any circumstances. Per All-In, Amanda McMaster cites data from Chinese quadrupeds already deployed here being back-channelled home, and wants a national robotics strategy. Trump is preparing import bans on advanced Chinese robots and power inverters. Note the split inside the same debate: Mark Zuckerberg argues the US should not ban Chinese AI models.
  • Botswana did everything the textbook asks and the miracle still stalled. Per Cremieux, its diamond output fell from $4.7 billion in 2022 to $1.98 billion in 2025 as lab-grown stones went from one in eight American diamond purchases in 2019 to 61 percent, and it has posted its first back-to-back years of GDP decline.
  • The Senate advanced a sweeping Russia and Iran sanctions bill on the day of Lindsey Graham's funeral, and confirmed Jay Clayton as Director of National Intelligence. American Airlines briefly grounded every US flight on a systemwide IT outage.
  • Keiko Fujimori was sworn in as Peru's first elected female president, its ninth in ten years. Chad withdrew from the International Criminal Court.
  • The wealthiest one percent of American earners now own half of all US equities, and the administration is ending a $3.6 billion Medicare drug-plan subsidy.
