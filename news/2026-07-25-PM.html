This morning the Houthis said they had no intention of closing Bab al-Mandeb, and by this evening they had put three ballistic missiles into a Saudi refinery and a loaded supertanker had turned around before the strait

Bahrain and Kuwait secretly sent jets into Iran, their first direct retaliation, while China moved to mediate and Iran signalled willingness, which knocked oil down 3 percent on a day everything else went the other way. Brent has gone from $71 on July 1 to $98.30, and prediction markets put a strike on Iran by July 31 at 55 percent.

I led this morning on ship insurers pulling war coverage and gave the Houthi assurance about Bab al-Mandeb its own paragraph. That assurance is already worthless. Ranked.

1. The war hit a refinery, and the shipping lane voted with its rudder. The escalation is physical now. Per Lark Davis, the Houthis struck Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery with three ballistic missiles. Jazan is Saudi Arabia's fifth largest at 400,000 barrels a day, and multiple columns of smoke were rising off it. On his read Bab al-Mandeb is now effectively shut and the Saudi route to China stretches from 19 days to 50, which he calls an unprecedented level of crisis in the global economy.

Then there is the evidence that does not depend on anyone's interpretation. Per staunovo, the empty supertanker New Champion, chartered by a Chinese buyer to load crude at Yanbu, U-turned in the Gulf of Aden on Friday just short of Bab al-Mandeb and is running east at about 16 knots. A ship that has already been paid to make a voyage does not turn around on a press release.

The state actors escalated too. Per The Spectator Index citing the Wall Street Journal, Bahrain and Kuwait secretly sent jets to attack sites inside Iran, their first such direct retaliation against Tehran, alongside strikes on Yemen's Hodeidah port, sirens in Bahrain, and the United States disabling a second vessel running the Iran blockade. Per Anas Alhajji, more than 40 tankers laden with Iranian oil are now sitting off Malaysia.

2. China is mediating, Iran says it is willing, and the oil tape believed it. The counter-track is real and it moved a price. Per amitisinvesting, Reuters reports China is trying to mediate and Iran is willing, and five minutes after that crossed, Trump posted that China told him they will never let Iran have a nuclear weapon, adding "if they sell them any weapons, they better watch out." Oil fell 3 percent on the session, which he reads as traders sensing the escalation may have a chance to stop.

Hold both facts at once, because the professionals are not agreeing with the tape. Insurers pulled war coverage on Saudi cargoes and a chartered supertanker physically turned around on the same day the oil price fell on a mediation headline. Insurers and shipowners are pricing escalation with their own capital and they move slower than a futures curve.

The betting markets are with the insurers. Per threadguy, Polymarket had a US or Israeli strike on Iran at 22 percent by the 25th and 55 percent by the 31st, with B-1s on alert in Britain, F-16s out of Germany, F-35s from England and tankers massing in Israel. The market's chosen expression of all this is Lockheed Martin, up 20 percent in two days, which he attributes bluntly to "cost per kill."

3. The Fed has a seven-week-old chair, a stablecoin deadline today, and a 38 percent chance of hiking on Wednesday. Per Anas Alhajji quoting the Financial Times, CME futures now price a 38 percent chance of a quarter-point hike Wednesday, up from 13 percent a week ago, with BofA's Mark Cabana saying the meeting is "definitely live" and pointing at oil. Per StockMKTNewz, Polymarket has it at 75 percent hold and 25 percent hike, with a cut at zero in both venues.

Kevin Warsh has been in the job seven weeks. Per WOLF, he told Congress the Fed is "racing" to meet the GENIUS Act stablecoin rulemaking deadline that falls today. Per Milk Road, he has launched five task forces to rebuild how the Fed collects and weights data, pulling in outside figures including Marc Andreessen, on the argument that the official inflation series does not match what people actually pay.

Their guest makes a case worth hearing that Warsh's hawkish debut was theatre. A new chair inheriting inflation running near 3.5 to 4 percent has no credible way to open dovish, and he pointedly declined to submit a dot-plot projection. The person to watch instead is Christopher Waller, who has changed his tune and now hints the funds rate may be a little too low. The threshold offered is clean: one hike and done is survivable, a signalled series is not.

4. The CLARITY Act now has a $13 trillion asset manager pushing it and a bank lobby quietly killing it. Per WatcherGuru, Charles Schwab called on the Senate to pass the Crypto Clarity Act. Per Lark Davis, CNBC reports it goes to a full Senate floor vote before the August recess, with endorsements now spanning bankers and unions including a police union this week.

The most honest description of the blocker came from Circle's president. Per WOLF: "Banks have real problems with it, and some of that might be that they sense you guys are stepping on their air hose." That is a competitive objection wearing a prudential costume, and it explains why endorsements from police unions and asset managers keep arriving while the odds keep falling.

The politics are uglier than the lobbying. Per the Crypto list, Travis Kling says the bill died on the floor over ethics provisions after Trump pulled in $1.4 billion of crypto profits in 2025, while Anthony Scaramucci, prefacing it with "I'm the last guy in America to carry water for Donald Trump on ethics," argues Democrats won real concessions and the Senate should pass it anyway.

5. Around the world, quickly.

  • The House voted to ban members of Congress and their spouses from trading individual stocks. Per Trader Mayne, there were 198 no votes, all of them Democrats, and Nancy Pelosi voted against it. His read is that "her edge, her alpha, is gone." It still has to pass the Senate, so nothing is law yet.
  • Maine's Democrats nominated Troy Jackson to face Susan Collins, with Kalshi giving him a 65 percent chance in November, per the Kalshi list. The same feed has Haley Stevens at her best odds since September in Michigan at 39 percent, and JD Vance now tied with Marco Rubio for the 2028 presidency.
  • The United States is reportedly preparing a computer simulation to test mobilising a national draft, months before automatically registering men aged 18 to 26, per the Polymarket list. That is the kind of item that reads as procedural until you notice what it is rehearsing.
  • Canada signed a trade deal with the UAE as Mark Carney pushes to reduce reliance on the United States, per the Polymarket list. The same digest has China fining Trip.com $770 million, Spain declaring a wildfire state of emergency, and Nigeria adding 28,000 soldiers and four army divisions.
  • The dollar has lost close to a third of its purchasing power in six years, per the Kalshi list, and BofA projects a $2 trillion deficit this year carrying $1 trillion of annual interest, per the Financial Markets list. Those two numbers explain more about the Fed's problem than any single inflation print.
  • Alphabet's Waymo is considering ending its Uber robotaxi partnerships and launching solo in Austin and Atlanta in January 2028, per the Market list citing the Financial Times. Uber hit new 52-week lows on it.
  • The case for a sovereign equity fund got its best two-minute argument. Per Prof G Markets, the point is not redistribution but insurance: debt is unconditional and taxpayers owe the same interest whichever way the economy goes, while equity in AI companies is state-contingent, so in the world where AI eats the economy ordinary people own a slice of the upside. The skeptical half of the same conversation notes that buying the index now sends 40 percent of your money to seven or ten companies, so indexing has quietly become stock picking.
