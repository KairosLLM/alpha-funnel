The FCC banned new foreign humanoid robots, and China ships eighty-five percent of them

Brendan Carr's rule reaches quadrupeds too and carries a waiver process, the Unitree G1 has gone out of stock on Walmart, and eBay finally settled the cockroach case for about $56 million.

WorldPoliticsMarkets

The robot import ban landed today, and it did not come from where anyone said it would. Ranked, starting there.

1. The instrument is an FCC equipment rule, not a presidential import order. Per Diet TBPN, the Federal Communications Commission announced restrictions on new foreign-made humanoid and quadruped robots, with chair Brendan Carr framing it as securing "America's critical supply chains" against "connected foreign-made devices that could become part of critical American infrastructure."

That mechanism is worth understanding, because it explains both the reach and the limits. The FCC's lever is equipment authorisation for connected devices, so it can touch imports at all only because these machines have radios in them. Will Maniatis notes it is a published rule under statutory authority with a waiver process and judicial review attached. That is a meaningfully different animal from informal pressure. No effective date was given.

I flagged this thread yesterday off Boston Dynamics calling for exclusion and reports that Trump was preparing import bans. The correction is that what actually shipped is an FCC rule, and its scope is wider than the framing suggests. Dean Ball reads it as covering advanced robotics from all foreign countries rather than China alone. Nobody has resolved that ambiguity.

The numbers explain why this is being fought now. Roughly 15,000 humanoid robots shipped worldwide in 2025. Unitree and Agibot delivered about 5,000 each. Tesla and Figure shipped a few hundred between them, if that. China holds something like 85 percent of the market and most of the supply chain underneath it. Unitree units were on sale at Walmart for tens of thousands of dollars, and every Unitree listing there now reads out of stock.

The sharpest point in the segment is a comparison. Nobody has organised to defend Chinese humanoids, because no American company has built a business on top of one yet. With Chinese open-weight models the opposite is true, since firms already claim inference savings of up to 80 percent and cloud providers already earn revenue serving them. A ban is cheap when it has no domestic constituency. The Associated Press notes the timing lands ahead of a planned September meeting between Xi Jinping and Trump.

2. eBay settled the harassment case, and the conduct is worse than the number. I could not source this figure yesterday and left it out. It is about $56 million, of which $48 million goes to David and Ina Steiner, $6 million to charitable organisations, and $1 million from former chief executive Devin Wenig personally to a free-speech charity named for Ina Steiner.

The Steiners ran EcommerceBytes, a trade blog covering eBay. In 2019, angered by the coverage, eBay staff sent them a box of live cockroaches, a bloody pig's-head mask, a funeral wreath and a book on surviving the death of a spouse. Employees mailed adult magazines to neighbours in the husband's name, posted online invitations for strangers to come to the house for sex, travelled to Massachusetts to vandalise the home, and stalked the couple around their own town. Court documents show Wenig texting another executive that it was time to "crush" them and to "take her down."

Seven employees have already served prison terms of one to five years and eBay paid a $3 million criminal penalty. This settles the civil case the Steiners filed in 2021, roughly seven years after the campaign. One arithmetic note: the itemised components total $55 million against a $56 million headline, so treat the breakdown as the reliable part.

3. The Iran war escalated overnight and a Russian missile landed in Poland. Per the Polymarket AM list, Central Command completed a heavy wave of strikes on dozens of Revolutionary Guard targets inside Iran at 10pm Eastern on Wednesday. Army HIMARS batteries in Kuwait fired ATACMS and PrSM into southern Iran, with impacts at Abadan, Ahvaz, Bushehr, Kish Island and Bandar Abbas. More than 50,000 American service members are now deployed across the region.

Per the Polymarket list, Central Command spent Thursday rebutting three Iranian claims point by point: that three American F-35s were destroyed at an air base, that the tanker M/T Nora broke the blockade, and that Hormuz is unsafe because of US action. It says none of the aircraft were hit, the tanker did not break through, and the threat to shipping is the Guard's own. Separately an Iranian missile killed a worker in a Chinese firm's building in Kuwait.

Two quieter developments carry more signal than the strike count. Qatar sent its first LNG cargo through the Strait of Hormuz in more than three weeks, the Al Aryesh, loaded at Ras Laffan in early July. And the Pentagon has committed more than $120 billion to expand Patriot missile and submarine production. That is the direct answer to the interceptor-depletion problem I flagged yesterday. Britain is putting £8.4 billion into nuclear submarines alongside it.

In Europe, a suspected Russian cruise missile crossed into Polish airspace and crashed near Targowisko, leaving a ten-metre crater. The European Commission president called it an unacceptable violation of EU airspace.

4. CLARITY is running out of calendar, and the odds have noticed. Seven days remain before the Senate leaves. Per Lark Davis, sixty votes are still unconfirmed with roughly ten Democrats reported on board, and Thom Tillis and Ruben Gallego have finalised a bipartisan ethics compromise whose details are not public and which still needs White House approval.

The problem is floor time. John Thune will only cancel the August recess for the SAFE America Act, and only if he gets fifty votes for budget reconciliation with SAFE attached. Meanwhile Cynthia Lummis has rejected the latest rewrite outright, arguing Republicans have already conceded nearly everything Democrats asked for. Kalshi prices passage this year at 36 to 40 percent, and JPMorgan has now said in public that the longer approval slips the greater the threat to crypto markets.

Lummis is also the one making the honest argument for urgency: "90% of the spot market is overseas. 80% of the digital asset futures market is overseas. They'll stay there."

5. Around the world, quickly.

  • South Korea's retail wipeout is a generational event. More than 360,000 margin accounts were forcibly liquidated with 62 percent of them under 35, and 1.2 million accounts were called. Circuit breakers fired on both main indexes two days running for the first time simultaneously.
  • US federal debt passed 100 percent of GDP for the first time since the Second World War, per the Kalshi list.
  • All 55 UEFA member associations agreed to boycott the next men's and women's World Cups over Gianni Infantino's plan to sell a stake in a new for-profit entity holding FIFA's media and commercial rights. UEFA's line: "The World Cup belongs to football and will never be for sale."
  • Amazon's Zoox won the first US approval for paid robotaxi rides with no steering wheel or pedals, on the same day the administration announced new steps to accelerate self-driving deployment.
  • ABC alleges in a filing that the administration is retaliating over its news coverage through an early licence review, which it says sends a warning to every other broadcaster.
  • Japan cut its 2026 growth forecast from 1.3 to 0.9 percent, citing oil prices driven by the Iran crisis.
