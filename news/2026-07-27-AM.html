The bombing pause has a named cause now, and it is Omani officials who flew to Tehran on Friday, with the munitions shortage confirmed at the level of the Joint Chiefs

CBS says the halt followed the Oman trip. CNN says JD Vance and General Dan Caine warned Trump against escalating, with Caine citing depleted US munitions. Both are true and Sunday's reporting had only one of them. Polymarket now puts a signed ceasefire this month at 55 percent.

Sunday I reported the strikes stopped because America is running out of Patriot interceptors. Overnight the diplomatic half arrived and the munitions half got a far better source. Ranked.

1. The pause is now explained twice over, and the two explanations are the same story in the right order. Per the Financial Markets list, CBS News reports the US bombing in Iran was paused because Omani officials travelled to Tehran on Friday for talks. Oman is the mediator, named and singular. Neither Pakistan nor Qatar appears this morning, so the joint proposal I relayed Sunday is being advanced by nobody today.

The supply side got the upgrade that matters. Per the Polymarket list, CNN reports Vice President JD Vance and Joint Chiefs Chairman General Dan Caine warned Trump against escalating, with Caine citing depleted US munitions. On Sunday that was analyst inference off a New York Times stockpile figure. This morning it is the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs telling the President not to escalate because the magazines are empty. Per the Financial Markets list, NBC reports the military has been forced to let some Iranian projectiles through its own defences, though that carries no figures and should be held loosely until it does.

The market took the relief seriously. Oil fell 7 percent at the open and Polymarket now prices a signed US-Iran ceasefire this month at 55 percent. Read the order of causation before buying the calm: a country that stops firing because it is out of interceptors is negotiating from a worse position than one that stops because it won, and Tehran can read CNN.

2. The attacked-ship story flipped continents and flipped blame. Sunday's vessel struck a naval mine in the Strait of Hormuz. This morning's is a different ship in a different sea with a different accused party. Per the Polymarket list, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi says Ukraine attacked an Iranian commercial vessel in the Caspian Sea, killing one sailor, calling it "a blatant UN Charter violation done at Israel's behest to drag Europe into its war." He warned in calls with EU High Representative Kaja Kallas and Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov that it "cannot go unanswered."

Kyiv argues the opposite direction. Per the same digest, Zelenskyy claims evidence that Russia helped Iran target American military bases. Two governments accusing each other of secretly serving the other side of the same war is what the end of a conflict looks like when nobody wants blame for restarting it.

3. Bab el-Mandeb has partly reopened, and this is the first hard shipping number in a week. Per the Financial Markets list, the Arabic energy outlet Attaqa reports two shipments of Saudi oil have departed via Bab el-Mandeb after days among the tankers stranded in the Red Sea, with one of the two carrying more than two million barrels of Saudi crude. The report calls it a limited improvement in navigation traffic rather than a resumption.

Take the modesty of that framing seriously. Two vessels moving is not a corridor reopening, and Sunday's edition had an Iranian spokesman saying the fighting had already reached this strait. But it is the first physical evidence in either direction, and that beats the freight-rate speculation standing in for it.

4. A CIA whistleblower went on the record the night before Fauci testifies, and the agency contradicted him by name. Per the People to follow list, Catherine Herridge published an interview with James Erdman III, a former Senior CIA Operations Officer, who alleges intelligence-community "globalists" feared Trump would disrupt their system. His words: "They're worried that President Trump might do something crazy that upsets what we have decided is how the world should work. And frankly, we got globalists working inside the IC, last I checked, we work for America."

His allegations run wider than the quote: that the CIA moved to block his own COVID-19 Senate testimony, that directed-energy attack records were destroyed, and that investigators working for former Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard were spied on. All one man's account, none of it corroborated this morning.

The agency answered on the record, and that is what makes this reportable rather than merely loud. CIA Director of Public Affairs Liz Lyons: "There is no disputing CIA's record of successfully executing on President Trump's transparency initiatives. Any insinuation that CIA was an obstacle and not a facilitator is wrong." Herridge notes no response from Gabbard, from the ODNI, from Dr Anthony Fauci or from Peter Daszak. Fauci is scheduled to testify before Senator Rand Paul on Wednesday, which puts the Fauci hearing and the Fed decision in the same news cycle.

5. It is 100 days to the midterms and Kalshi's own book disagrees with itself. Per the Kalshi list, its traders give Republicans a 57 percent chance of controlling the Senate. In the same post its seat forecast reads Democrats 49, Republicans 47 with 7 toss-ups, and both named Senate markets favour Democrats: Sherrod Brown 51 against Jon Husted 49 in Ohio, Troy Jackson 62 against Susan Collins 38 in Maine. A 57 percent chamber probability on top of a seat count with Democrats ahead is not a forecast, it is two desks that have not spoken. Kalshi also has Kamala Harris running in 2028 at 75 percent, an all-time high.

Against that, per Election Time, a state-by-state map predicts Republicans 53 and Democrats 47 in the Senate with governors at 28 to 22, naming two Senate flips that cancel out, North Carolina to the Democrats and Michigan to the Republicans. Be blunt about what that is. He names no pollster in 24 minutes, shows no model, no sample sizes and no margins of error, cites his own aggregation site as the sourcing, and never covers the House. The most useful thing in it is a Kalshi price, Republicans at 45 percent to flip the Wisconsin governorship against 16 percent three months ago.

One absence to record. The 51 percent Democratic sweep I reported Sunday appears in none of this morning's sources, so treat it as unconfirmed.

6. Around the world, quickly.

  • Delhi police called their handling of India's biggest protest in years "professional", per the People to follow list, while The Economist reports video of officers attacking lone protesters and assaulting women, under a headline about Modi's party discovering the limits of propaganda.
  • A gold-bar corruption case has set Indonesia's army against its police, per the same digest, two institutions that both have allies in government and have rarely gone after each other's leaders.
  • The bulk carrier Golden Leo capsized off Ukraine's coast, per the Polymarket list, a week after three Russian cruise missiles hit it on 19 July. Ten killed including four Indians, eight rescued. Russia also cancelled its Navy Day fleet parade in St Petersburg for the second year running.
  • Israel approved a multinational security force entering Gaza under Trump's proposed peace plan, per the same digest, which also has former Honduran President Juan Orlando Hernández home after Trump pardoned his 45-year US drug-trafficking sentence.
  • Pete Hegseth's plan to routinely screen soldiers for low testosterone has no precedent in any other major military, per the People list, and The Economist's judgment is that it is unusual rather than preposterous.
  • Trip.com rose nearly 8 percent after China closed its antitrust investigation with a $765 million penalty, per the Polymarket list. A fine that lifts the stock 8 percent is a settlement priced as a release.
  • BitMart is shutting down with withdrawals open and almost nobody using them, per the People to follow list. Only 58 wallets withdrew in 24 hours for about $805,000, and none have processed in eight hours. An exchange closing quietly with unmoved customer funds is a consumer story before it is a market one.
