Day 145 of the Iran war brought a thirteenth straight night of American strikes, drones over Kuwait and a missile at a base in Jordan, while an Omani delegation quietly landed in Tehran to talk about who controls the Strait of Hormuz

Iran's foreign minister says there is no ceasefire until its Hormuz demands are met, and JPMorgan thinks gasoline prices are close to the level that drags Trump to the table. The White House published a plan to rewire $200 billion a year of federal science funding, two congressmen introduced a kill switch for frontier AI models, and a conservative case for universal healthcare arrived with a proposal to defund anything obesity-related.

The war is now widening geographically while the diplomacy starts moving underneath it, and those two facts belong in the same paragraph. Ranked.

1. The war widened to Kuwait and Jordan, and the first real negotiating channel opened at the same time. Start with where the fighting actually is. Per the Polymarket list, CENTCOM struck Iran for the thirteenth consecutive night with more than 30 strikes hitting Ahvaz, the Revolutionary Guard says it hit the Al Azraq base in Jordan where seven missiles and six drones were downed, Iran is firing drones and missiles at Kuwait, and a Romanian F-16 shot down a drone over its own airspace.

The diplomacy moved on the same day. Per the Financial Markets list, foreign minister Araqchi said Iran will not accept a ceasefire until its demands on the Strait of Hormuz are met, and an Omani delegation arrived in Iran to discuss managing ship traffic through it. Per the Kalshi list, the war is 145 days old and Marco Rubio said the United States should have attacked sooner.

Follow the fuel price to find the pressure point. Per the Financial Markets list, JPMorgan's commodities desk argues gasoline prices are approaching the level that pushes Trump toward talks with Iran, the Houthi negotiator Mohammed Abdusalam describes the blockade as limited to the Saudi side, and some crude grades already trade above $110. Per the Polymarket list, Saudi Aramco is offering extra Mediterranean cargoes because Red Sea risk is rising, and Trump says Xi and Putin personally assured him they will not sell weapons to Iran, warning it would be "very bad for them."

So the cash markets are trading pure escalation while the odds markets and the shuttle diplomacy point the other way. The tradeable side of that gap sits in today's Investing edition.

2. The White House published the first serious rewrite of federal science funding since 1945, with a knife in the same drawer. This one deserves more attention than it will get. Per Packy McCormick, Michael Kratsios released Science: A New Golden Age on Tuesday, 123 pages explicitly modeled on Vannevar Bush's 1945 Endless Frontier, arguing that the peer-review system allocating roughly $200 billion a year works so well it has calcified.

The diagnosis is concrete. Researchers spend nearly half their time on administration, more than 270 new grant requirements were added between 1991 and January 2025, and some grants take 20 months from submission to award. The proposals include long-horizon funding on the Howard Hughes model (about $10 million over seven years, cited as producing high-impact work at nearly twice the rate of federally funded peers), fast grants decided in under a month, "golden tickets" letting a single reviewer force through one unconventional proposal, and DARPA-style program managers.

Now the part that changes its meaning. A separate proposed budget-office rule would let political appointees block grants before they are issued and terminate awards already running. A plan to free scientists from paperwork, arriving bundled with a mechanism for political control of who gets funded, is one document doing two very different things. The clock is real either way, since the FY2028 research memorandum requires agency implementation plans within 90 days.

3. Two congressmen introduced a kill switch for frontier AI, and the open-weights fight turned openly political. The bill is narrow and serious. Per the Hermes list, Representatives Ted Lieu of California and Nathaniel Moran of Texas introduced the AI Kill Switch Act, giving Homeland Security authority to order an emergency throttle or shutdown of frontier models, with penalties up to $20 million per day.

It landed in the middle of a lobbying war. Per the Stocks list, two dozen companies including Nvidia, Meta, Microsoft, IBM, Palantir and the Linux Foundation signed a letter arguing that open model weights are a safety strategy rather than a risk, carried by Jensen Huang's first-ever post on X, and OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and xAI all declined to sign. Erik Voorhees reduced it to a slogan, "Anthropic / OpenAI / Trump Administration vs All other Americans." The technical substance of that letter is in today's AI & Tools edition.

The most credible safety argument of the day was about neither. Per Prof G Markets, the nearer risk is engineered biology rather than a self-improving superintelligence, the scenario being a jailbroken model asked to generate doomsday virus candidates that are then ordered from a lab. The line that should end the argument about which country to worry about: "a virus made in Turkmenistan can kill humanity just as easily as a virus made in Massachusetts."

4. A conservative case for universal healthcare arrived, and its own proposal is the problem. The diagnosis is stronger than most on either side. Per American Philosophy, American healthcare is already socialized and badly, with Medicare and Medicaid alone consuming 26 percent of the federal budget, the United States spending over $1,000 per person per year on administration alone against $200 to $300 in peer single-payer systems, ten administrators for every provider, and 97 percent of metro areas exceeding the federal threshold for a highly concentrated inpatient market.

The "no right to another's labor" objection gets answered with precedent rather than sentiment. Emergency treatment is already compulsory under EMTALA, and Powell, Betts and Gideon made legal counsel a constitutional necessity delivered by public defenders who are paid professionals rather than conscripts. The family-formation numbers are the emotional core: an uncomplicated birth averages $15,712 and about $20,000 with the newborn, surgical miscarriage care runs $9,000 to $14,000 billed uninsured, and 52 percent of American women aged 20 to 39 had never given birth in 2024 against 36 percent in 2006.

Then it proposes abolishing the VA, Medicare and Medicaid, and sorting every ailment into self-imposed and circumstantial, with the self-imposed category (including anything obesity-related, a comorbidity in 60 percent of hospital admissions) receiving zero public funding and pooled only with other self-imposed patients. Congress would write the list into statute and courts would settle the edges. An argument that spends thirty pages establishing that risk pooling is how the healthy subsidize the sick then builds a system that un-pools the sickest people in it.

For what it is worth, the definitional fight underneath all of this got settled cleanly elsewhere. Per Prof G Markets, socialism historically means state control of the means of production, so redistribution is a different thing, and the Scandinavian countries are less regulated and more capitalist than the United States on the production side while redistributing far more on the other.

5. Around the world, quickly.

  • LeBron James signed with the Philadelphia 76ers, two years and $8 million with a player option, per the Financial Markets list. Per the Kalshi list, Philadelphia immediately repriced to Eastern Conference favorite at 21 percent over the Knicks at 20. An $8 million deal moving a title market is a statement about the discount he took, and someone on the Heat's side allegedly staged a press-conference livestream to move the odds first.
  • India forced GitHub to take down Bitchat, per the Crypto list, on the stated grounds that it "significantly impedes interception, attribution, and investigation by law enforcement." It is already mirrored on Radicle, so the takedown demonstrated the exact property it objected to.
  • Governor Kathy Hochul paused data center construction in New York, per Noah Smith on Prof G. Per this digest, the organized local case against them is job loss, permanent underclass and an industrial monstrosity in the backyard for nothing in return. Every gigawatt in every AI buildout thesis has to clear a county board that reads it that way.
  • Skyroot put Vikram-1 into orbit, India's first privately developed orbital rocket, per Packy McCormick: 22 meters, 350 kilograms of capacity, customer payloads to 450 kilometers. India opened the sector to private companies in 2020, so a group of former space-agency engineers reached orbit in six years.
  • The Clarity Act got marked down 50 percent to 40 percent inside two hours on the Senate floor's mood, per the Kalshi list, after Senator Bill Hagerty said "the time is now" and then Trump ethics concerns stalled it. Per the Crypto list, Goldman's David Solomon broke with the other Wall Street banks to back it and the Fraternal Order of Police endorsed the amended version, which leaves two weeks before recess.
  • Rebuilding Gaza is now estimated at $71.4 billion, seven times all prior reconstruction efforts combined, per the Polymarket list. The same digest has China pledging to raise wind and solar generation more than 50 percent over five years, and Brazil becoming the first country to accept tokenized cattle as loan collateral, where a Paraná farmer borrowed $19,600 against ten dairy cows.
  • Elon Musk says he got "too involved" in politics, per the Kalshi list, on a day his rocket company's stock trades 47 percent below its June peak. Meanwhile Michigan's Democratic Senate primary has Abdul El-Sayed at 77 percent over Haley Stevens at 23, and Republicans hit a 45 percent chance in the Wisconsin governor's race, their best since March 2025.
