Last Sunday the story was that the market refused to price the Iran war, and this week it started pricing it through the one channel nobody can talk down, the insurance on a ship

Brent went from $71 on the first of July to $98.30 on Friday, sulfur is up 146 percent and wheat 15, and the Houthis put three ballistic missiles into a Saudi refinery hours after saying they had no intention of closing Bab al-Mandeb. Bahrain and Kuwait secretly flew strikes into Iran, and China is now trying to mediate.

WorldPoliticsMarkets

Last Sunday's memo argued that the most striking thing about this war was the market's refusal to price it, with crude sitting near $84 through six nights of strikes. That claim is now dead, and the way it died is the story. The war stopped being a headline and became a line item on a cargo manifest. Here is the arc, the transmission into prices, the diplomacy, and what to watch.

The arc of the week. It opened with the line everyone had been waiting for someone to cross. Two American service members were killed and one reported missing at Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Azraq, Jordan, on a base that had been struck four times in five days, with Black Hawks damaged. Pete Hegseth said their sacrifice "only stiffens our resolve." Trump ordered CENTCOM to open the gates of hell. By Wednesday a third American was dead, named as Sergeant Angel Rampersad, and Jordan was intercepting Iranian missiles with explosions near Aqaba and Eilat.

The chokepoint arithmetic was already brutal before any of that. Hormuz traffic had fallen to roughly 14 crossings a day from 34 in early July, Gulf oil exports to about 16 million barrels a day from 24 million before the war, and American petroleum inventories had drained 184 million barrels since it began. Iraq signed 48 deals with US companies worth more than $60 billion, one of them a pipeline to bypass the strait entirely. That is the kind of infrastructure decision that assumes this lasts.

Monday delivered the whipsaw that defined the week's trading. Houthi spokesman Yahya Sarieh declared a naval blockade on Saudi Arabia, "siege for siege." A tanker caught fire near Hormuz, two Greek tankers were attacked, Bahrain was hit, and sirens sounded across Bahrain and Kuwait. Then Iran reportedly proposed a ten-day ceasefire and crude crashed below $80 after trading over $90 that morning. President Pezeshkian told Iranians the country was in full-scale war.

Midweek the escalation became structural. Trump said the United States was not finished at all with Iran, that Iran had not seen anything yet, and that the fortified Pickaxe Mountain nuclear site would be hit very heavily. Robert Pape of the University of Chicago put the odds of American ground troops entering southeastern Iran that week near 70 percent, describing roughly 8,000 staged in the Gulf of Oman, 4,500 to 5,000 Marines plus about 4,000 from the 82nd Airborne, aimed at a symbolic coastal objective near Chabahar or Jask. He rates the odds of success low and does not advocate it. That window has now closed without confirmation, and it should be held against his number rather than quietly forgotten.

Thursday the Houthis hit two Saudi tankers, the ENCELIA and the LAYLA, with ballistic missiles and drones, and the IRGC warned Britain that any base it lends the Americans is a legitimate target. Marco Rubio said an eye for an eye had become a head for an eye and declined to rule out civilian infrastructure. Brent went back above $100 for the first time since May, up 42 percent in 20 days.

Friday Iran rejected the American ceasefire offer outright and threatened to destroy all oil and gas infrastructure in the region, with Kharg Island and the South Pars field named. Iran's parliament speaker said Hormuz will not return to its pre-war conditions. The war widened again, with 30-plus strikes on Ahvaz, the IRGC claiming the Al Azraq base in Jordan, Iranian drones and missiles aimed at Kuwait, and a Romanian F-16 downing a drone in its own airspace. That was day 145.

The transmission, and this is where the week actually turned. The insurance market moved first. Per Anas Alhajji, ship insurers restricted war coverage for Saudi Arabian cargoes in the Red Sea, and his reaction was two words, "OMG! It happened!" because this is the step he had spent a week warning about. An underwriter withdrawing cover is not rhetoric. It is a price that every subsequent voyage has to clear.

Then the physical evidence, the part no press office can spin. Per Staunovo, the Hong Kong-flagged supertanker New Champion, empty and chartered by a Chinese buyer to load crude at Yanbu, idled in the Gulf of Aden and then U-turned on Friday just short of Bab al-Mandeb, and is now running east at about 16 knots. A vessel that has already been paid to make a voyage does not reverse course on a rumour.

And then the refinery. Per Lark Davis, the Houthis put three ballistic missiles into Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery, Saudi Arabia's fifth largest at 400,000 barrels a day, with multiple columns of smoke rising off it. On his read Bab al-Mandeb is now effectively shut and the Saudi route to China stretches from 19 days to 50. Hold that against what the Houthis' own negotiator Mohammed Abdusalam had said that same morning, that there is no closure of Bab al-Mandeb and the blockade is limited to the Saudi side. The assurance was worthless inside twelve hours, and Alhajji had already noted dryly that even the Saudi side needs defining.

Brent itself went $71 on July 1, sat near $84 for two weeks, and finished Friday at $98.30 per the Market list. Note one honest gap: threadguy read Brent at 92 the same day, down off highs of 95, and each figure appears once, so the level is genuinely uncertain inside a few dollars even if the direction is not.

The offset almost nobody is naming belongs to Kpler's Matt Smith, who points out China has stopped buying and cut imports by more than 5 million barrels a day, which absorbed much of the Middle East loss and pushed the pain into refined products instead of crude. That is why sulfur and diesel and jet fuel moved more than Brent did, and why the strategic reserve sits near 300 million barrels, the lowest since 1983.

The last link is the one that reaches every reader's mortgage. Per the Financial Markets list, CME futures now price a 38 percent chance of a quarter-point rate hike on Wednesday, up from 13 percent a week earlier, with Bank of America's Mark Cabana saying the meeting is definitely live, that policy may not be restrictive, "and oil is now rising again." Per the People list, Polymarket puts the same hike at 25 percent. A cut is at zero in both venues. A war over a strait in the Gulf of Oman is now an argument about American interest rates.

The diplomacy, four threads and three of them alive. An Omani delegation arrived in Tehran on Friday to negotiate mechanisms for managing Hormuz traffic, the most concrete channel anyone has opened, and it runs straight into foreign minister Araqchi's condition that there is no ceasefire until Iran's Hormuz demands are met. A Pakistan-brokered track, described by Reuters through three Pakistani sources as a China-initiated push to resume US-Iran talks, was the headline that pivoted markets intraday on Friday. Zerohedge's read on it was appropriately deflating: talks about talks.

China's own mediation is the newest and the most interesting. Per amitisinvesting, Reuters reported Beijing trying to mediate with Iran willing, and five minutes later Trump posted that China had told him they will never let Iran have a nuclear weapon, adding that if they sell them any weapons they had better watch out. He has separately said Xi and Putin both personally assured him on weapons sales. A mediator who is also the buyer of last resort for sanctioned Iranian crude, with 40-plus laden tankers parked off Malaysia, has leverage no Western capital has.

Congress spent the week losing the argument twice. The House passed a war-powers resolution for the second time on Thursday with four Republicans crossing, and the Senate rejected its version with Susan Collins the only Republican in favour. The same $1.15 trillion defense bill added $60 billion, much of it tied to this campaign.

What to watch, with dates. Trump meets Netanyahu on Tuesday. The Fed decides Wednesday, and given Cabana's own framing, oil is now an input to that decision rather than a sideshow. Per threadguy, Polymarket puts a US or Israeli strike on Iran by July 31 at 55 percent, having priced 22 percent for a strike by the 25th, and the tells he reads out are the ones that cannot be concealed: B-1s on alert in Britain, F-16s out of Germany, F-35s from England, and refuelling aircraft massing in Israel. Tankers are always the giveaway.

Beyond the dated items, three questions decide whether this gets worse. Whether Bab al-Mandeb stays shut to Saudi-linked hulls now that the Houthis have shown they will hit refineries. Whether insurers restore Red Sea war coverage or more chartered vessels quietly refuse the route, because that is the mechanism that converts a regional war into a global tax. And whether China's mediation is real or is leverage, which will be legible from whether Iranian crude keeps moving to Malaysia while the talks proceed.

One counter-signal deserves its place, because a memo that only stacks evidence one way is propaganda. Cornell, Northwestern and other American universities plan to resume in-person classes across the Middle East despite the conflict. Institutions with lawyers and insurers of their own are not behaving as though the region is closing. Set that against the tanker that turned around, and you have the honest shape of the week: the professionals nearest the water are pricing escalation, and the professionals one step back are not.
