The Fed held, three officials dissented for a hike, and the thirty-year broke out

Kevin Warsh said there is no soft inflation target, the S&P turned green at 2:47pm and gave every bit of it back for a trillion-dollar day, and Benjamin Cowen's call that a hold forces the long end higher landed inside 24 hours.

MacroStocksBitcoin

Last night I said the thing to watch was not the decision but the thirty-year the morning after. That took less than an afternoon.

Market context. The Fed held the target range at 3.50 to 3.75 percent, a fifth straight hold, and the vote is the story. Per the Polymarket list it went 9 to 3, with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan all dissenting in favour of a hike. Per the Stocks list that is the most dissents since 2016, and every one of them wanted rates higher.

Kevin Warsh then removed any comfort the hold offered. He said the committee remains resolute on price stability and that "there is no soft inflation target because the target is 2%." On killing forward guidance he was plainer still: "Market participants are learning to play the ball, not the referee." Andreas Steno's read was blunt. "Warsh is the driver of the tech sell-off."

The tape agreed and then disagreed with itself inside forty minutes. Per the Kalshi list, almost $400 billion was added to the S&P 500 in the five minutes after 2pm, and by 2:47pm the index was green on the day. It ended with more than a trillion dollars erased. QQQ went 680 to 663 into the bell and printed $656 after hours, the Nasdaq 100 is more than ten percent off its peak, and the S&P is tracking its worst July since 2007.

The long end is where the damage actually is. The thirty-year yield had been trying to clear 5.2 percent since October 2023 and broke through today, its highest since before the 2008 crisis. Jeff Park dated it precisely, the highest since July 31, 2007.

The scoreboard.

  • Hit. My hold call at 78/22 landed, and so did the hawkish-statement leg at 55/45. Warsh gave the market the pause and none of the relief.
  • Hit inside 24 hours, and not mine. Benjamin Cowen said last night a hold is what triggers the revolt and would push the thirty-year through 5.2 percent. It did, today. The rest of his thesis, a September hike and a 10 to 20 percent drawdown, runs to end-September and stays open.
  • Miss, and not mine. Citadel Securities flipped its forecast to a hike and was wrong. Polymarket never took the hike above 26 percent.
  • Hit, and not mine. Bernstein's Gautam Chhugani and Piper Sandler both modelled roughly $150 million of Robinhood event-contract revenue. It printed $156 million.
  • Due inside two sessions. GDP and June PCE tomorrow morning, Apple and Amazon tomorrow after the close, Strategy's Q2 on Thursday.

1. The Fed got less hawkish on the front end and the bond market revolted anyway, and that contradiction is the trade. Per Cowen, market-implied odds the Fed is still at 3.75 percent in September rose from 23 percent before the release to almost 43 after, so September hike odds fell from roughly 77 to 57. That is a dovish repricing. Meanwhile Michael Bento left the press conference saying "this sounded very hawkish" and now expects a September hike, and the thirty-year broke a three-year ceiling.

Both are true once you separate the instruments. Warsh made the next meeting less certain and the inflation target less negotiable. Killing forward guidance cuts the odds on any single meeting while raising the premium demanded for thirty-year paper, because the buyer no longer gets told what comes next. Per the threadguy stream, meetings used to sit at 90 to 98 percent certainty a week out. This one sat at 75.

My lean, and it is a change. I am 60/40 the next move is a hike and 55/45 that it lands in September, revised down from the near-certainty the odds implied on Monday. Warsh has now shown he will accept three dissents rather than move early, and a chair who tolerates that much internal disagreement is not being rushed by it.

2. Good numbers kept getting sold, and tonight the market finally said what it is actually pricing. Per the Milk Road note, the pattern was already two for two. Alphabet grew revenue 24 percent with cloud up 82 against a 67 percent expectation, and fell more than 5 percent on raised capex and its first negative free cash flow ever. SK Hynix printed the biggest quarter in its history, revenue up 257 percent and profit up 557 at 76 percent margins, and fell 9 percent on a 6 percent miss caused by capacity rather than demand.

Microsoft also extended data-centre useful life from 15 to 25 years, which cuts annual depreciation on the same assets by roughly 40 percent and lands straight in reported earnings. Amit Kukreja put the tape in five words. "The street hates capex." That is not quite it. The street hates capex it cannot see the end of, and it paid up today for the company that drew a line.

3. The memory trade has floor prices underneath it now, and the tape does not care yet. Micron has signed 16 multi-year take-or-pay agreements. Per Rick Orford, 14 carry about $100 billion of cumulative revenue at minimum contract prices, plus $22 billion of expected deposits, and most of the largest include explicit floor prices. Per his longer piece they cover roughly 20 percent of DRAM volume and a third of NAND, against a reported quarter of $41.5 billion at an 80.4 percent operating margin and 9.2 times earnings.

Now the other side. Per Chip Stock Investor, Jason Hall's point is that memory names are price takers, so "they can look the cheapest right when they are the most expensive," and the tell that supply is coming is ASML calling memory a huge opportunity to place machines while Micron guides its own capex up.

The floor prices break the tie for me. A fifth of DRAM volume contracted at a minimum price is the first structural answer anyone has given to what happens when the cycle turns, and Hall's argument, for all its mechanical elegance, arrives with no numbers attached. I stay 65/35 that memory multiples expand before they compress, unchanged from Saturday, and the thing that flips it is ASML order detail rather than another week of red candles.

4. Korea stopped being a chart and became a retail solvency event. The KOSPI is down roughly 40 percent from its high, more than 360,000 margin accounts have been wiped out, and Korean retail has lost 56 trillion won, about $38.7 billion, on leveraged products. The finance minister apologised publicly.

Two facts sit against that. Samsung posted record Q2 operating profit of $61.7 billion, up 1,814 percent. And per Milk Road's panel, the CXMT listing that triggered the unwind may not deserve the read-through it got, because CXMT runs zero EUV layers against Samsung's seven and cannot make high-bandwidth memory at all for lack of an advanced logic die.

5. Torsten Slok has the best explanation for why the AI trade cracked now, and it is a credit story. Per Prof G Markets, Apollo's chief economist notes hyperscalers shifted financing from equity to debt and credit default swap prices hit record highs on Oracle, SpaceX, Google, Amazon and now Nvidia. These are among the most profitable companies alive, so the widening is not about default. It is the market repricing when the AI payoff arrives, and slower future cash flow means a lower equity price today.

His diversification math is the uncomfortable part. The ten largest stocks are 40 percent of the index and 87 percent of venture capital is now AI, so the same bet sits in public equity, public credit and private markets at once. Nine of the twelve biggest contributors to the S&P 500's first-half return were semiconductors.

The bull rebuttal is specific enough to weigh. Per the Stocks list, Gavin Baker argues spot GPU rental prices run at least twice contracted rates, so hyperscalers are underearning and whoever signed compute contracts in 2024 or 2025 is overearning. Dwarkesh Patel supplies the number underneath it, with Google reportedly paying $900 million a month for 110,000 GPUs rented from SpaceX at roughly twice spot.

6. Bitcoin refused to move all month, and the most bullish voice in crypto just named his floor. Per the Crypto list, BTC sat either side of $64,000 through the Fed, pinned in a $4,000 range for nineteen days, with a month low of $62,206. Long-term holders control a record 79 percent of supply and the fear gauge reads 28.

Per the Ansem interview, the man who called himself more bullish than ever gives a band rather than a slogan. The top of the range is $85,000, he was certain $60,000 would break and it did not, and his floor is explicit: "I really don't think we're going to break 58 to 60." His invalidation is clean at $40,000, which he says would mean SOL near $40 and another four to six months of waiting. He also names who defended the level. Michael Saylor sold $200 million at $60,000 after buying every top for five years, the ETFs were sellers too, and quiet institutional money took the other side.

Hold that band against the book. It contradicts Nansen's $52,000 to $58,000 retest call, answers Zach Pandl's open question about a $58,000 bottom, and sits inside Novogratz's $60,000 to $85,000 range for the year. Three independent people now bracket the same zone, and the argument is down to about two thousand dollars on the low end.
