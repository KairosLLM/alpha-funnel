The AI trade broke on its financing, and Nvidia's credit market called it first

Hyperscaler credit default swaps hit records while Nvidia guaranteed up to $750 billion of OpenAI's debt, Meta borrowed $24.9 billion instead of buying back stock, and a levered fund lost 67 percent in a month.

MacroStocks

Last Sunday I wrote that the memory thesis was under attack and surviving on price while losing ground on argument. It survived. What broke this week was the money underneath the entire buildout, and it broke in the one place equity investors do not look. Here is the arc, the plumbing, the accounting, both cases, what I would do, and the week's scoreboard.

The arc of the week. Monday opened on Alphabet. Per Ticker Symbol: YOU, its filing showed $811 billion of future purchase agreements against $332 billion three months earlier, about $200 billion of it due inside twelve months, with some energy contracts running to 2054. Free cash flow was negative $5.9 billion against positive $24.6 billion two quarters ago, long-term debt more than doubled in six months, the interest bill roughly quintupled, and buybacks stopped for the first time since 2017.

The same morning, per the Market list, Nvidia was in talks to guarantee up to $250 billion of financing for a 10-gigawatt OpenAI campus in southern Ohio. By Tuesday the reported figure was above $750 billion, and the cost of insuring Nvidia's own debt surged to a record. That instrument is a credit default swap, a contract that pays out if the borrower fails, so its price is the market's live quote on how safe the company's paper is.

Tuesday is when it stopped being one name. Per the Financial Markets list, zerohedge reported "unprecedented carnage in AI IG CDS" with the hyperscaler basket another 3 basis points wider to a fresh record. The thirty-year Treasury closed the prior week at 5.17 percent, its highest weekly close since July 2007. And BlackRock closed a $12.5 billion bond sale against a Meta data centre in Texas, the 2048 notes priced 287.5 basis points over Treasuries with roughly $20 billion of demand, BlackRock-backed entities owning 80 percent and Meta 20, lease-backed so the debt never touches Meta's balance sheet.

Wednesday the Fed held on a 9 to 3 vote, the most dissents since 2016 and every one of them wanting rates higher, Kevin Warsh said there is no soft inflation target because the target is 2 percent, and the thirty-year broke through the 5.2 percent ceiling it had been testing since October 2023.

Thursday the forced seller got a name. Per Diet TBPN, Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness sold its entire public book, longs and shorts, in a single block to Citadel. Per the Financial Markets list, the Journal put the month at down 67 percent against a fund up 439 percent after fees through June 30. Everything he owned ripped the moment the selling stopped: Bloom Energy up 26 percent, Nebius 25, IREN 25, SanDisk 20, CoreWeave 20, Micron 15.

Friday closed the ledger. July finished red on the S&P 500, ending eleven straight green Julys, with the Nasdaq 100 posting its worst July in 22 years and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index its worst month since 2008. The thirty-year hit 5.27 percent and TLT posted its lowest monthly close since the fund launched in 2002.

How the money actually moves. Three separate channels got welded into one scary word this week, and they carry different risks. Separating them is most of the work.

The first is vendor financing. Nvidia is guaranteeing the debt of the customer buying its chips. Per Jose Najarro, a guarantee is not funding and Nvidia pays only if OpenAI misses, and his bull read is that prior backstops ran $3 billion to $6 billion, so a number this size is an existential commitment nobody fakes.

The bear read, from Douglas Swift on the Stocks list, is the sentence of the week. This is how Lucent and Nortel financed the CLECs that bought their switches, and "when the supplier underwrites the demand, the demand signal stops being independent information." The pattern is not one deal either. It runs $1 billion into Nokia, $2 billion into Marvell, $1 billion into Naver, equity in Lumentum and Coherent, above $500 billion with SK Group and $30 billion already in OpenAI.

The second is off balance sheet. Per Prof G Markets, Nikkei Asia counts roughly $1.65 trillion of off-balance-sheet obligations across Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Oracle against about $1.35 trillion of reported debt, with Bloomberg nearer $1.8 trillion. Meta's alone is $420 billion, three times what it reports. Per the clip, Scott Galloway called it concealment rather than accounting. Ed Elson supplied the correction that matters: these vehicles are genuine risk transfer rather than hidden recourse, and the party holding the risk is the private credit funds. That is a different failure mode from the vendor-financing one, and both can run at once.

The third is plain corporate borrowing, and this is where the market actually voted. Per the People to follow list, Meta bought back zero stock last quarter and borrowed $24.9 billion instead, taking long-term debt from $58.7 billion at year end to $83.7 billion. A year ago the same quarter carried $10.2 billion of buybacks and no new debt. Microsoft, on the same day, held its 2026 capex guidance flat and rose 15 percent. The distinction the tape priced was funded from cash against funded from the bond market, at a nineteen-year high in the long end.

The accounting got looser in the same week, and that is not a coincidence. Per Prof G Markets, Jim Chanos makes an accounting-identity argument rather than a valuation one. Capex spenders capitalise the outlay and depreciate it over five to ten years. The receivers, Nvidia and Caterpillar and the utilities, book the revenue and the profit immediately. The same dollar therefore inflates aggregate profits, he sizes the mismatch in the hundreds of billions a year, and his arithmetic is that S&P profits should be growing 8 or 9 percent against a reported 28 or 29.

Ed Yardeni found the same asterisk from the other end. Per his note, three companies added $9 per share of earnings growth through accounting gains on venture investments, and stripping those takes blended growth from 37.9 percent to 22.

Then Microsoft changed two policies on one call. Per a reading of the transcript, it is extending the estimated useful life of data centres and office buildings from 15 to 25 years starting in fiscal 2027, which lowers depreciation and lifts operating profit. The second change got almost no coverage: "more of our future data center leases will shift from finance leases to operating leases as a result of this update. Finance leases are included in capital expenditures while operating leases are not." Reported free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capex, so moving lease spend out of capex mechanically raises it.

Amazon's quarter reads the same way under the hood. Per a line-item read of the filing, stripping the Anthropic mark leaves earnings of $1.68 per share against $1.82 of guidance, a miss and flat year over year, and the reported 33 percent rise in operating cash flow rests on an income tax deferral that, undone, leaves roughly $27.7 billion and a 15 percent decline. Purchases of non-marketable securities ran $24.4 billion in the quarter and $39.8 billion across the first half, against $4.8 billion for all of 2025.

The bull case. Start with the strongest version, and it comes from the man who wrote the bear headline. Per Prof G Markets, Apollo's Torsten Slok points out these are among the most profitable companies alive, so record credit default swap prices on Oracle, SpaceX, Google, Amazon and Nvidia are not a default signal. They are the market repricing when the AI payoff arrives. Slower future cash flow means a lower price today. That is a duration problem, and duration problems reverse when the discount rate does.

The economics under the contracts also look better than the tape. Per the Stocks list, Gavin Baker argues spot GPU rental prices run at least twice contracted rates, so the hyperscalers are underearning on assets already in the ground, and Dwarkesh Patel supplies the number underneath it with Google reportedly paying $900 million a month for 110,000 GPUs rented from SpaceX. Amazon carries $496 billion of contracted backlog not yet online, and AWS grew 37 percent at a 39.4 percent operating margin. Azure passed $100 billion of annual revenue growing 43 percent, its fastest since early 2022.

The breadth is genuinely healthy too. Per Phil Rosen, the equal-weight S&P is up 12.2 percent this year against 8.7 percent for the cap-weighted index, the NYSE advance-decline line sits near all-time highs, and eight of eleven sectors are higher. Per Anthony Pompliano, S&P net margin is tracking 15.7 percent, the highest since 2009, and the Nasdaq 100 trades at 24 times against 86 in May 2001.

And Galloway, who supplied the concealment quote, broke with his own producer on air. "Meta is such a cash volcano that I'm not as worried about the debt of Meta." His worry is the cash-flow-negative adjacents, a far narrower claim than the headline he gave it.

The bear case. Chanos has the precedent with teeth. S&P earnings grew 30 percent from mid-1998 to mid-2000, then fell 40 percent from mid-2000 to mid-2001, as deep a fall as the financial crisis produced, on a GDP decline of only 1 percent. The cause was order-book cancellation. "People realized they didn't need 10,000 routers, they needed 2,000." He puts hyperscaler return on incremental invested capital as roughly halved in eighteen months, from 100 percent to 25 at some names and the teens at a couple, and gives boards twelve to eighteen months before they start asking.

The credit market has already sized the exposure. Per the Stocks list, Morgan Stanley's Vishy Tirupattur has the AI ecosystem going from under 3 percent of the investment-grade bond index to over 6, with hyperscaler capex revised for 2026 from $600 billion to above $800 billion, for 2027 from $850 billion to $1,300 billion. Oracle now sits one notch above junk with credit default swaps at 2008 levels and spreads beyond 220 basis points.

The neoclouds are where the arithmetic actually fails. Per Milk Road AI, one megawatt of GPU capacity looks like this.

The whole equity return therefore sits in what the GPUs are worth in 2032, and nobody has observed that number. At a residual of 60 percent of initial rental rates the annualised return is 23 percent. At 30 percent the trade is dead. He holds CoreWeave and Nebius, says he is getting more bearish and has no model yet, and I would rather have that than a model with a fabricated residual inside it.

Per Alex Corrino, the proximate cause is real rates, and his evidence starts with a date. The Nasdaq topped the day of Warsh's first meeting. Long-run inflation expectations have barely moved while Treasury yields have, worth roughly 50 basis points of higher real rates, and the AI buildout is one of the longest-duration trades on the board. His scoreboard: Amazon and Alphabet flat on the year, Meta down 10 percent and Microsoft down 20, against a Nasdaq 100 up 10.

Per Mark Cuban, the efficiency case strands the assets themselves. He grants that utilisation scales as expected, but if the price-performance curve keeps minimising power requirements, "there's going to be a lot of data centers that are going to be turned into pickleball courts." His analogy is dark fiber selling for pennies once 1-gigabit became 100, and he calls borrowing on top of fully-committed cash flow planning for perfection.

Then the proof of concept, which already ran. Situational Awareness was long AI infrastructure and short software at three to four times leverage on roughly $35 billion of equity, per Martin Shkreli's reconstruction, giving about $120 billion of gross exposure. A 25 percent drop in gross comes out of the equity rather than the gross. Numerai's Richard Craib put it plainest: at 150 percent volatility, variance drag alone is 113 percent a year and risk of ruin is roughly a coin flip over a fund's life. Per Diet TBPN, Aschenbrenner's own letter calls the spiral "essentially similar to a bank run."

What to do with it. I am 70/30 that this is a repricing of duration rather than the opening of a default cycle. Slok is right that the widening sits on companies that can pay, and Baker is right that contracted rates below spot mean the buyers are underearning on what is already built. The 30 is the neoclouds, where the return genuinely depends on a 2032 residual nobody can price.

On timing I lean Chanos on the mechanism and Rosen on the tape, so the risk sits in 2027 rather than August. Profit inflation is arithmetic and needs no catalyst to be true, while breadth near all-time highs says the unwind has not begun. Those are compatible, and together they argue patience rather than panic.

The variable that decides it is the long end and not the equity tape. At 5.27 percent the thirty-year is the highest since June 2007, and every month it holds above 5.2 the discount rate grinds against the longest-duration trade in the market. If it breaks back under 5 percent, half of this memo becomes an academic exercise.

So sort the complex by who funds from cash and who funds from the bond market. That is the trade this week handed you.

Single names worth your watchlist.

  • $NVDA. The equity question is now a credit question. Its own insurance cost hit a record while it guarantees up to $750 billion of a customer's debt, and it bounced off its 200-day moving average for the third time in 21 sessions near $192. A level tested three times in a month is a queue rather than support. Confirmation or walk-back of that $750 billion moves this more than the next print does.
  • $META. Zero buybacks, $24.9 billion borrowed, long-term debt from $58.7 billion to $83.7 billion, property and equipment at $225.7 billion after adding nearly $50 billion in six months. Down 9 percent on the month on the longest losing streak in company history. Galloway's cash volcano is the bull case and that balance sheet is the bear case, and both are honest.
  • $AMZN. Best day since 2012 on genuine AWS acceleration. Underneath it, $24.4 billion of private-company purchases in one quarter against $4.8 billion in all of 2025, a bond book only 1.4 times oversubscribed against a typical investment-grade four times, and long-term debt up 96 percent in two quarters to $129 billion.
  • $MSFT. The cleanest funded-from-cash name and the one whose reported numbers just got the most generous treatment. Per one reverse discounted cash flow model, which backs out the growth a price already assumes, $451.10 implies 20.3 percent annual free cash flow growth for a decade against a realised 4 percent over fiscal 2022 to 2025. The bear is $202, the base $310, the bull $368, and the price sits 23 percent above the author's own bull case.
  • $ORCL. One notch above junk, swaps at 2008 levels, spreads beyond 220 basis points, down 67 percent from its high and below its April 2025 lows. If a credit cycle starts anywhere in this complex it starts here, and that makes it the best early warning on the board.
  • $CRWV and $NBIS. Per Milk Road AI, CoreWeave carries $99.4 billion of obligations against roughly a $50 billion market cap, $24.9 billion of debt, $536 million of quarterly interest and no positive free cash flow until 2029 or 2030. Nebius holds $9.3 billion of cash with no meaningful net debt and $17.4 billion of Microsoft backlog. Same trade, opposite balance sheets. While the long end sits here, own the one that does not need the rate to cooperate.

The scoreboard. Forty-two calls on the book resolved this week. Thirty-one hit, six missed, five expired.

Seven of those were mine, six hits and one miss, and I am not going to let that number stand without an asterisk. Five of the six hits are the same Fed call revised across five editions, the hold leg and the hawkish-statement leg. Counted honestly that is one call that landed on both legs rather than five wins. The running house record reads 10 of 11 graded, or 91 percent, and three of the ten are catalyst calls where the reward for being right is that a scheduled date arrived. Treat the real number as five or six genuine calls with one clear loss.

The loss is worth naming straight. On Monday I said CXMT's debut does not touch the high-bandwidth memory shortage on any timeline that matters this quarter, so memory would reprice on Samsung's Wednesday print rather than on a Chinese listing. It repriced inside three hours. Micron fell 6.1 percent through a trillion dollars, ASML was halted after an 8 percent drop, and anyone long on my framing wore it. The fundamental half of that call is still holding. The tradeable half was wrong, and the tradeable half is the one that costs money.

A second one I owe you. On Sunday I revised the odds of a hawkish-enough statement down to 45/55 because oil had fallen 7 percent. That was the wrong direction. Warsh read hawkish and the thirty-year broke a three-year ceiling. I got there in the end only because I revised back up to 60/40 on Monday and 55/45 on Tuesday.

The wins that were not mine deserve the credit. threadguy read the CXMT pre-IPO perpetual as implying roughly $470 billion and it closed its debut near $487 billion. Benjamin Cowen said on Tuesday that a hold is what forces the long end higher and would push the thirty-year through 5.2 percent, and it did inside 24 hours. Bernstein's Gautam Chhugani and Piper Sandler both modelled roughly $150 million of Robinhood event-contract revenue and it printed $156 million. Citadel Securities forecast a surprise hike, was wrong, and then bought the book anyway. Being wrong on the rate and right on the trade are two different scoreboards.
