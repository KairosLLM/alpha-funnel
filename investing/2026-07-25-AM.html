Per the Polymarket list, the S&P turning red was explicitly an Iran headline. Per the Kalshi list, the Nasdaq 100 is heading for its worst July since 2004 while Apple sets records inside the same index.

The dispersion underneath is historic. Per stocktalkweekly, this is the biggest one-month drawdown for momentum stocks on record according to both the Morgan Stanley and Goldman momentum indexes, from a trader who is still up 160 percent this year and was up 584 percent over twelve months. Owning the index cost you very little in July. Owning what worked in June cost you everything.

2. Institutions bought the dip on Friday afternoon and retail has stopped buying entirely. The flow data contradicts itself in a way that resolves once you split it by who. Per the Kalshi list, $680 billion was added back to US stocks in the last two hours of Friday and investors are "flooding into US equities at a record pace." The same feed, nine hours earlier, said retail investors are buying at the slowest pace in more than six years, which per Barchart is a six-year low.

Both are true and they describe different people. A late-Friday two-hour institutional bid into a tape where households have gone quiet is the definition of a market held up by professionals, and per asklivermore the average retail portfolio is down 12.7 percent this year. That is why they are quiet.

3. Rates are the transmission channel, and the 30-year above 5 percent is doing all the damage. Every bearish chain this weekend runs through the bond market rather than through AI demand. Per LuxAlgo, the 30-year yield has spent more time above 5 percent than at any point since the financial crisis. Per Lisa Abramowicz, real yields on the 10-year are the highest since 2023, and Milk Road Macro reads a 2.3 percent real yield as monetary strangulation arriving without another hike.

Per Melvin, the chain is mechanical: Iran escalation lifts oil, oil keeps inflation sticky, sticky inflation keeps yields high, and high yields compress the multiple on every long-duration name. That is why Nvidia, Micron, Nebius, SanDisk, Broadcom and Applied Optoelectronics all sold as one basket. He does not think there is a hike this year. Note the oil number is genuinely in dispute, with Chamath saying crude costs $81 and having fallen from $100, against desks the same evening describing oil as pushing $100. I am not building anything on a level two credible sources put $20 apart.

The important part is that nobody is calling the fundamentals broken. Per Melvin, the AI infrastructure thesis has not suddenly broken and what changed is the multiple investors will pay. Per Milk Road, Robinhood at the same $100 stock grew revenue 53 percent to $4.5 billion and platform assets 48 percent to $377 billion, so prices fell and the business did not.

4. Memory is the most crowded fight in the market, and Burry pressed his shorts into it. The longest-term conviction in the market sits directly opposite the worst near-term chart. Per Shay Boloor, Michael Burry added to his Micron short at $934 and his Nvidia short at $210, and per the Kalshi list his Tesla short is up 24 percent since June 30.

The other side is institutional and enormous. Per BlackRock's Tony Kim speaking to Molly O'Shea, the firm is modelling a trillion dollars of capex this year and $10 trillion over five, the "primacy of memory" becomes more important from here, and only three companies control it. Per The Tech Investor, Nvidia and SK hynix announced a $500 billion initiative including a 2-gigawatt data center running Vera Rubin chips on HBM4 memory (the stacked high-bandwidth memory that every AI accelerator needs), and IREN sits on $7.6 billion of cash against a market cap barely twice that.

My lean, and it is a lean rather than a conviction: Burry has the next month and Kim has the next three years. The specific line that settles it is Micron reclaiming 955, and until that happens the structural bulls are early and saying so out loud.

5. Five independent desks arrived at the same posture, and that agreement is the loudest thing in the data. Nobody is selling and nobody is buying. Per the Stocks list, Chairman's Ledger is "a little less long and finger off the trigger on dips, no selling but not adding," PD Philly is risk-off with 10 to 20 percent cash, Ariel calls it chop, and Jim Cramer says he is struggling to find reasons to buy, four days after saying "Intel's the one."

The disagreement is about depth and it is genuinely tradeable. Per that same list, vnkumarvnk points out the S&P is barely 3 percent off its all-time high and warns a 20 percent drawdown is still ahead, TicTocTick says the bear market is already here, and Ariel names the actual arbiter: the Nasdaq proxy reclaiming its 50-day, or a 10 percent-plus correction followed by a real follow-through day. Anything in between is chop.

I lean chop over bear market at roughly 60/40, because the equal-weight tape and the S&P are both within a few percent of highs while only the momentum cohort has broken. That view dies the moment the Russell loses its 50-day after four red weeks. That is precisely where it sat on Friday.
