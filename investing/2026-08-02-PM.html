1. The strait is closed and the volatility market has priced none of it. Per the Market list, Iran says reports it agreed to reopen the Strait of Hormuz are false. A source cited by Fars News says no deal was accepted, the strait stays closed while the US continues what Iran calls hostile actions, and ships may pass only via Iran's designated route with permission from the IRGC Navy.

Per the Polymarket list, Iran is contradicting Trump's claim directly, CENTCOM has redirected 35 vessels, and Ukrainian drones hit the Saratov refinery overnight.

So the shooting risk fell and the shipping risk did not. That is the distinction the tape is missing, because a closed strait keeps working without a headline.

My lean, and it is a change from Friday. I am 65/35 that oil is higher two weeks out, up from roughly even, because the de-escalation everyone traded was about strikes rather than transit. The 35 is that Iran has reopened before under this much pressure.

2. The unwind was the worst on record, and that is the bull case rather than the bear one. Per Jordi Visser, Goldman's VIP index of the most crowded hedge fund positions fell 12 percent for the month against the S&P through Wednesday. That is the worst relative month since the index began in 2001, worse than the month of Lehman, and roughly double any other month on the record.

Seven of those 12 points came in four days, and that four-day rate of change is the worst ever measured, including COVID. It happened in a tape where the S&P barely moved and the VIX stayed low. Goldman had flagged the fuel: the buildup in gross leverage over the first five months was the largest cumulative increase since it began tracking in 2016, from an already all-time high.

Visser has been inside three of these. He took over the S&P book during LTCM, was positioned through the 2007 quant unwind, and had thirty-odd portfolio managers reporting to him through Archegos. His read is a cleansing event at high probability, and the reasoning is specific rather than hopeful. Hedge funds are still up more than 10 percent on the year, so the chip stack is positive and the rational move is to hedge and come back rather than liquidate.

3. This was not an AI crash, and the tape proves it. Per Porter Stansberry, the consensus post-mortem on Aschenbrenner blames four-to-one leverage. His objection is that leverage is a magnifying glass rather than a verdict, and nobody has explained why the positions were wrong. Then he shows you. Here are both legs of the book from the June 30 close to the July 29 close, the last session before the block trade cleared it.

Over the identical twenty sessions the QQQ fell 10.14 percent, the SPY fell 2.32, and Nvidia fell 5.04 and still finished July up 0.33. The S&P sat near its record the whole way. So the index did not break. The market rotated violently out of the leveraged, capital-hungry end of the AI complex and into the profitable, asset-light end. That is out of exactly what he owned and into exactly what he was short.

Stansberry's second point is the one with money in it. Adobe trades near 11 times trailing earnings, Salesforce 13, Intuit 14, on operating margins of 36.6 and 28.7 percent and capex around 1 percent of revenue. Those are the multiples of a dying industry applied to businesses selling AI as an upsell, with Microsoft's contracted backlog at $678 billion, up 84 percent, and 30 million paid Copilot seats against 15 million in January.

The funding side agrees. Per Phil Rosen on the Financial Markets list, capex at Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta rose from $150 billion in 2022 to $358 billion in 2025 while their combined free cash flow fell 15 percent to $199 billion. Per the All-In Podcast, David Sacks calls the move momentum rather than fundamentals and expects a return on the capex.

My lean. Sacks is right that the selloff was leverage and wrong that it says nothing, because Stansberry's table is not a leverage story, it is the market repricing who captures the value. I am 70/30 that the July low holds into mid-August, raised from 60/40 on Visser's evidence, and 60/40 that asset-light software beats the buildout names over six months. That second one is the trade this week actually handed you.

4. UBS put the biggest number yet on memory, and the chart has not confirmed it. Per the Market list, UBS projects hyperscaler memory spending more than doubles to $761 billion in 2027, reaching roughly 75 percent of total capex, with shortages extending into the summer of 2028 and only three suppliers controlling the market. Hyperscalers are locking supply through prepayments and multiyear agreements.

The conclusion is what matters for positioning. If that means years of elevated earnings rather than one good cycle, memory becomes a multiple-expansion story, and UBS has $MU, SK Hynix and Samsung moving from roughly 5 times earnings to nearly 12.

The shortage has reached Apple's shelves. Per Bloomberg via the People to follow list, Apple faces major MacBook Air shortages on the memory and silicon crunch, with many new orders not arriving until September. A market tight enough to delay the best-supplied hardware company on earth is a demand signal rather than a narrative.

The tape has not agreed yet. Per the Stocks list, AskLivermore sees no confirmation of a low in $MU or $SNDK, though the MACD histogram shows seller exhaustion, and he expects a few more marginal lows first. Dan Niles goes further the other way: the semiconductor index had two separate drawdowns above 50 percent in the 1990s and this one is about 25 percent off the peak, which he calls nothing against an index that still ran 850 percent into March 2000.

5. The Treasury Secretary called crypto a national priority and the Senate gave it a 4 percent chance. Per Lark Davis, the third principle of Scott Bessent's economic strategy speech was this: "Digital assets, stablecoins, tokenization, and the new payment systems will help shape the future of money. The United States should not consign itself to the sidelines while that future is built elsewhere." Mohamed El-Erian called it a remarkably important speech.

Set that against the legislature. The Senate has five days before recess and Kalshi prices CLARITY passage at 4 percent, down from 33 for the year and 40 a week earlier.

Benjamin Cowen thinks the whole argument is a distraction. Per his latest, Bitcoin sits further below its fair-value logarithmic regression trend than at any time since 2010, and the four-year cycle says weak for a few more months yet. His prescription is to stop focusing on ETFs, strategic reserves and the CLARITY Act and start fixing fundamentals. Kalshi's traders are blunter, forecasting Bitcoin crashes to $50,000 this year.

Self-custody also took a real hit. Per the Market list, Jonathan Goodman lost 18.25 Bitcoin in the July 29 ColdCard hack, from a device in a safety deposit box that had never touched the internet, after one line of 2021 seed-generation code was brute-forced with AI.

6. Coinbase built the rails for agent payments and earns almost nothing from them. On Friday I said nobody had covered $COIN and it deserved a look before the CLARITY vote. Per Milk Road on the Stocks list, here is that look. Revenue was $1.22 billion against roughly $1.29 billion expected with a GAAP net loss of $359 million, into a quarter when crypto spot volume fell 25 percent.

What got built underneath is the argument. Per Artemis data in Coinbase's own deck, more than 99 percent of onchain agentic commerce settled in USDC, more than 97 percent ran through x402 and more than 90 percent over Base. Brian Armstrong's framing is that there will soon be more AI agents than humans, and they cannot open bank accounts.

Single names worth your watchlist.

  • $MU. UBS at $761 billion of 2027 memory spend and a 5-to-12-times re-rating is the most aggressive framing published, and 906 still confirms the reclaim. Buy the thesis, wait for the level, and do not chase into Wednesday's $SNDK print.
  • $SPCX. First earnings report in company history Tuesday, days ahead of the August 6 unlock. Two events that both cut one way into a stock already 47 percent off its June high. Position size is the whole decision.
  • $TSLA. Finished last week with its entire weekly candle including wicks outside the lower Bollinger band, only the second time ever. The first was February 2016, after which it ran 80 percent in eight weeks. One instance is an anecdote rather than a base rate, so treat it as a setup and not a signal.
  • $COIN. At $141 you are paying for the exchange and getting the agent-payment rails for nothing. The CLARITY leg just got priced at 4 percent, so the near-term catalyst is gone and the option is longer-dated than it looked Friday.
  • $ADBE. Eleven times trailing earnings on a 36.6 percent operating margin, capex under 1 percent of revenue, and AI-first recurring revenue past $500 million after tripling. It was the short leg of the book that just blew up, and it rose 28 percent in July while the buildout names halved.
  • $AMD. Reports Tuesday, up about 15 percent off the lows. The read-through to memory pricing in its cost line matters more than the headline.
