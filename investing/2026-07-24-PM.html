Intel beat on every single line and handed the entire rally back by two in the afternoon, because it also raised capex to $20 billion and this market has started billing anyone who spends

Alphabet got the same treatment a day earlier and printed its first negative free cash flow in twenty years. The average stock sits a percent and a half from a record high while the index clings to support, some crude grades pushed above $110, and two desks called opposite bottoms in Tesla within an hour of each other.

This morning the question was whether Intel's blowout could carry the tape. By two o'clock the tape had answered, and the answer had nothing to do with Intel's quarter. Here is the session, then seven stories.

The close. A day of holding the line rather than repairing anything. Per Reddog, the S&P held $735, yesterday's low, and stalled under $744 as resistance, while the Nasdaq proxy lost and then reclaimed $686, the level Heisenberg had called extremely important support before the open.

The internals were the interesting part. Per Heisenberg, the volatility index actually fell 1.6 percent through the tech damage, and the equal-weight version of the index (the same 500 companies with each one counted the same, so the mega-caps cannot carry it) sits 1.5 percent from an all-time high. Alphabet rose 2 percent on a red day. Intel fell more than 10 percent from its premarket highs to close down over 4 percent, per the Kalshi list, and Tesla printed a new low at $315.

Underneath, the commodity tape kept tightening. Per Anas Alhajji, some crude grades traded above $110, and per the Kalshi list, wheat hit a three-year high. The one clean pop came at 3:16pm on a headline about a possible Pakistan-brokered channel to Iran, where InvestorsLive booked index calls up 1,200 percent. Crypto miners were the only green sector on the board, up 1.4 percent today and 27 percent on the week, per Blockworks.

The momentum screens are uniformly bad. Per Stocks with Josh, his hourly momentum crossover shows sell signals across almost the entire Magnificent Seven with $NFLX the lone buy, $MSFT and $GOOGL both below the zero line, and a diamond-pattern breakdown on the index itself. Per threadguy, the read is simply that stocks are "really toppy," with Tesla missing earnings, Alphabet's capex up sharply on negative free cash flow, and war risk back on the board.

1. Intel delivered the quarter and lost the day anyway, and the $20 billion capex line is why. The print was not the problem. Per the People list, $INTC earned $0.42 against $0.21 expected on revenue of $16.13 billion against $14.42 billion, and then raised full-year capital spending to $20 billion after signing three-to-five-year supply agreements. Hours later the same account posted the epitaph, "Aaaand, it's gone."

The mechanism was named out loud while it happened. Per the Market list, Hulk Capital wrote that Intel was up $12 after crushing earnings "but they are ramping capex and the market is punishing big capex spenders," and Jim Cramer spent the session on the other side, asking sellers to "come back and knock these two down for us" on Intel and $AMD.

So a company that spent two years being told to invest in its foundry got sold for saying yes. That is a regime, and Intel is now the cleanest example of it.

2. The market has flipped from paying for AI spending to charging for it, and Alphabet is the bigger tell. Look at what happened to the best cloud number of the quarter. Per the Market list, $GOOGL grew Cloud 82 percent with 36 percent operating margins and passed a billion monthly users on AI Mode, and a further $15 billion capex increase in three months pushed quarterly free cash flow negative for the first time in more than twenty years. Per Heisenberg, the stock is now under both its 50-day and 200-day averages for the first time since May 2025 and has fallen in 9 of the last 11 weeks. Per this note, that cloud line was $24.8 billion in absolute terms.

The rotation is explicit. Per threadguy, Alphabet is spending north of $200 billion "at a time where their AI sucks," and his division of the market is receivers good, spenders bad, with $MU, $MRVL, $BE, $SNDK, $INTC, $AMD, $ASML and $AVGO on one side and $GOOGL, $AMZN, $META, $TSLA, $MSFT and $ORCL on the other. $ORCL is down more than 53 percent in under two months, per Heisenberg.

Then there is the part of the bill nobody has to show you. Per Prof G Markets, roughly $1.7 trillion of data-center debt sits in special purpose vehicles (separate companies that hold the debt and the chips, which the hyperscaler only partly owns so it stays off the parent's balance sheet). Their named example is Hyperion, where PIMCO and Blue Owl hold about 80 percent and Meta about 20 percent while being the only client. Asked what happens to those investors if the data-center revenue never arrives, the answer was that they do not get made whole. Their line is the honest one, "the only reason this is not considered a problem is it hasn't actually happened."

3. Micron is the consensus dip-buy of the day, and for once the bulls brought levels and a date. Two unrelated desks landed on the same name. Per Joseph Hogue and Bridget of MarketBeat, $MU is the pick over $PLTR and $ORCL on 12 times trailing and roughly 6 times forward earnings with a 75 percent EBITDA margin (cash profit before interest, tax and writedowns) against a 45 percent five-year average, and 181 percent expected earnings growth. Bridget's version is blunter, "a 75 percent profit margin can't be faked."

The catalyst is dated. Both name hyperscaler earnings in the next one to two weeks as the moment memory capex either shows up or does not. Per Stocks with Josh, millions of dollars of next-week calls printed and Micron closed green on a red tape, with his trigger at 996, targets at 1,035 then 1,089, and a stop at 983.

The bear case has a name too. Per Hogue, Louis Navellier gives memory 12 to 18 more months of explosive growth before a rethink, and per the Stocks list, John Galt spent the day taking memory profits in $SNDK, $ALAB and $EWY after calling the face-plant. Kyle Reidhead is on the other side calling the 35 percent drawdown a gift, with semis at 19.5 times forward earnings, below their ten-year average.

4. Two credible desks called opposite bottoms in Tesla within an hour, and both showed their work. This is the cleanest disagreement on the board. Per Mentovia, $TSLA breaking 321 "was no good signal, and the crash is now accelerating," with 286 to 292 as the technical destination and 321 and 332 flipping to resistance overhead. Per TicTocTick in the same digest, "I am calling a low in Tesla stock here now 300," on the argument that Tesla at roughly 12 times sales is cheaper than Apple at the same multiple.

The fundamental read is worse than either chart. Per Prof G Markets, Optimus asks Tesla to ramp a brand new product on a brand new supply chain with a brand new design, and Tesla has no track record doing that at this complexity and cost. Per Stocks with Josh, who is short into $300, the last 14 percent single-day drop took 60 days to find its bottom before doubling, and he is hedging his own long-term position through the end of August rather than picking the low.

5. The macro data quietly turned stagflationary today and nobody connected it. Read these prints together instead of one at a time. Per Fast Stock News, the July flash composite came in at 53.6 with services beating at 53.6 against 51.5 expected while manufacturing missed at 53.8, and June new home sales beat at 628,000 but the median price fell 2.7 percent year over year to $398,300 against 9.3 months of supply, with the West down 22.4 percent.

Now the other side of the same economy. Per LuxAlgo, S&P Global reports US businesses charging their highest selling prices in four years. Per the Polymarket list, a Glassdoor survey of more than 1,700 professionals found 57 percent felt pressured to accept lower pay after losing a job. Per the Crypto list, Indeed job postings are down 3.5 percent year over year and 37.2 percent since April 2022, back to February 2021 levels.

Firm prices, soft labor, cracking housing, $110 crude and three-year-high wheat. Per Noah Smith on Prof G, the AI bubble does not need a demand collapse to break, it needs "token maxing" to meet a profit-and-loss statement, the same way factories in 1900 ripped out steam boilers for electric motors, saw no gain, and reverted before reorganized workflows finally delivered three to five times the productivity twenty years later.

6. The cash-flow lens explains this whole tape better than any AI narrative does. One retail investor did the most useful work of the day. Per this post-mortem, he sold his index fund over the inelastic market hypothesis, admits it cost him ("being wrong and being early look identical for a while"), then screened about thirty names on true free cash flow yield, subtracting stock compensation as well as capital spending. The results run $ADBE at 8.59 percent, $CRM at 7.76 percent, $FDS at 6.10 percent and $NVDA at 1.82 percent. His summary is the line of the day, "the cash machines got sold and the dream got bought."

Run today's wreckage through that lens and it holds up. Per this teardown, $ORCL sits near $125.84 against a 52-week high around $345, with record operating cash flow of $32 billion and a $638 billion order book on one side, and $40 billion of data-center capex plus $85 billion of debt on the other. Per this model, $META at $600.91 needs only 16 percent annual free cash flow growth to justify its price against 33.7 percent delivered, with the honest caveat that its fair-value range of $383 to $1,026 is nearly three times wide.

The bubble argument got a scoreboard too. Per Goldman's Top of Mind roundtable, Acadian's Owen Lamont names four horsemen (overvaluation, bubble beliefs, equity issuance, surging inflows) while conceding that IPO waves can run for years and that the absence of big first-day pops argues euphoria has not arrived. Apollo's Torsten Slok adds that IPOs since 2019 underperformed over the following three years. Goldman's own Ben Snider takes the other side, putting roughly $700 billion of 2026 issuance against a $75 trillion market.

Then compare the quality of argument on the bull side. Per this conviction post, the case is that world compute goes from about 132 gigawatts to a terawatt in a decade and "anybody talking about an AI bubble is an idiot" because the hyperscalers "have smarter people than you and I," expressed as 20 percent $SNDK and 15 percent $MU. Per Anthony Pompliano, the old hedge is broken anyway, with long Treasuries down almost 50 percent over five years so bonds now amplify equity moves instead of cushioning them, though his proposed answer routes into a paid placement and should be read on those terms.

7. Bitcoin is holding the mid-60s and the leverage behind it is dangerously lopsided. The level everyone trades around is explicit. Per Sykodelic, "we want to see $66k held on the weekly," or the $60,500 test he has watched for weeks comes into play, while aixbt already sees Bitcoin below $64,000. Per Scott Melker, the fact that it held the mid-60s while $800 billion came off AI stocks and oil ran at $100 is itself the argument.

The positioning is the risk. Per Stocks with Josh, there is roughly $1.5 billion of short leverage against $10 billion of longs, and he expects a grind up toward $69,000 to be where that long stack gets flushed rather than where it gets paid. Per Jaime Coutts of Real Vision, the high $70,000s is the weekly inflection and Bitcoin does not get there without exchange-traded fund flows turning positive, though Ethereum already shows a positive divergence with each price low pairing with less selling.

Per Benjamin Cowen, the base case is a stock-market top in August or September, a 10 to 20 percent drop, and the cycle low after that. He raises his own counterpoint honestly, that this cycle topped on apathy, so the sellers a capitulation requires may never have bought in the first place.

The policy and plumbing side is moving faster than the price. Per Brian Armstrong on Market Bubble, hundreds of large US companies integrated stablecoins within three months of the Genius Act, he spent two days in Washington whipping votes for the Clarity Act against opposition from banks, law enforcement and tribes, and tokenized equities including Nvidia, Tesla and SpaceX are coming to Base. The trader alongside him says the crypto bottom is already in with a bull market in three to six months, running 60 to 70 percent majors against 30 to 40 percent speculation.

Real onchain activity is inflecting even in a dead tape, and the venue has moved. Per threadguy, Pump.fun revenue hit its best day since June 30 with most volume on Robinhood's chain rather than Solana, driven by memecoins paired against tokenized equities, where a $GME coin squeezed the thin tokenized supply until Europeans minted fresh shares to arbitrage it back. Per ETH Labs, whose executive director openly concedes that ETH "had that incredible success 5 years ago and ever since it's sideways," the fast confirmation rule now near production in every major client cuts deposits and withdrawals from minutes to seconds. Per Lark Davis, XRP funds took in $1 billion with the price going nowhere, which he reads as early holders distributing into institutional demand as it arrives.
