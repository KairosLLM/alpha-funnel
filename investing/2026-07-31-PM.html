Eleven green Julys ended, and Aschenbrenner told his investors it was a bank run

The S&P finished July red without ever reaching 7,499.36, the Nasdaq 100 had its worst July in 22 years and semiconductors their worst month since 2008, and Amazon still posted its best day in fourteen years.

MacroStocks

The month that broke the AI trade also broke a decade-long streak, and the man at the centre of it says he was never liquidated at all.

Market context. July finished red on the S&P 500, ending eleven straight green Julys running back to 2015. Per the Market list, the level needed was 7,499.36 and it was never reached, with the same desk that set the line on July 25 confirming the outcome after the bell. The Dow closed its fourth green month in a row, so the two indices split.

Underneath, the damage concentrated exactly where you would expect. The Nasdaq 100 had its worst July in 22 years, per both the Kalshi list and the Financial Markets list. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index had its worst month since 2008. Momentum as a factor had its worst month in twenty years, recovering slightly in the final two sessions. The KOSPI ended the month down 20 percent after being down 40 percent at the lows.

Friday itself was a split tape rather than a rout. Amazon closed up 15 percent, its best day since 2012. Apple fell roughly 10 percent and handed the largest-company title back to Nvidia. Roblox fell around 27 percent on Q2 earnings, its worst day on record. Across the month the Magnificent 7 went Microsoft up 21 percent, Amazon up 12, Apple up 5, Nvidia up 2, Alphabet down 1, Meta down 9 and Tesla down 27.

The bond market is where the month actually happened. The 30-year hit 5.27 percent, its highest since June 2007 and 450 basis points above the 2020 low. TLT posted its lowest monthly close since the fund launched in 2002. Oil reclaimed $85 and finished July up 15 percent on Iran. And the VIX closed at its third lowest weekly close since January, into a weekend where strikes have reportedly been ordered.

The scoreboard.

  • Miss, and the level never came close. The S&P needed 7,499.36 to keep eleven green Julys. It finished the month red. Anyone reading a dividend-adjusted chart will see SPY green, so the split is worth knowing before someone tells you the streak survived.
  • Miss. Polymarket had a signed US-Iran ceasefire this month at 55 percent when I carried it on Monday. The month ended with an attack ordered instead.
  • Expired untested. Galaxy Digital's end-of-July catalyst passed without a report, and the $38 fair value against roughly $25 never came into play.
  • Not confirmed. I wrote this morning that Micron needed a 906 close to validate the reclaim. It closed down 5.90 percent instead, so the level stands untaken and the map is still live rather than proven.
  • Standing. My 60/40 that the July low holds into mid-August is unchanged, and the long end is still the thing that would move it.

1. The letter says it was a bank run, and it says the fund was never liquidated. Per Diet TBPN, Leopold Aschenbrenner wrote to his investors last night and the letter has been read out nearly line by line. It opens where it should. "We let you down this month. We came closer to permanent capital impairment than is acceptable to us."

His account of the mechanism is more specific than any wire report has been. "On Wednesday night, Thursday morning, we took decisive action to protect LP Capital. We traded a portion of our public portfolio in a block transaction to remove all leverage from the fund and prevent further losses. All shorts were closed and reliance on portfolio financing removed. We currently manage a fully paid for public book, long stock and long fully paid for options, with no margin liquidity risk."

Then the denial, which is the part that changes the story. "The fund was not shut down. It was not liquidated or transformed into a private only fund." He adds that virtually all of his own capital remains in it, and takes full responsibility. His diagnosis of the spiral is that "we started to see increasingly adverse trading in names publicly associated with us," and that "these dynamics are essentially similar to a bank run."

I have been calling this a blowup for three days and I should sharpen that. Selling a block to a willing buyer to meet a margin call is not a rescue and not a wind-down. It is a forced trade at a price you did not choose. The distinction matters because Polymarket's market on whether the fund unwinds has now flipped to no longer projected to unwind, per the Polymarket list.

Two things I am not going to print. Nobody has published the letter itself, and the year-to-date figure splits between roughly 80 percent as read on air and about 40 percent in the Financial Times rendering of the same document. And nobody has a price for the Citadel block. The two numbers circulating are a worked example offered as illustration and an unsourced "40 to 50 cents on the dollar," so treat both as noise.

One genuinely new fact does hold. Per the same source, the Wall Street Journal reports Situational Awareness agreed late Wednesday to sell a $3.5 billion Anthropic stake to a group led by Greenoaks and Sequoia, then walked away Thursday morning and sold the public book instead. That kills the widely repeated claim that he sold half his Anthropic position. He kept it.

The Friday tape on his named holdings was still ugly, and per the People to follow list most of them gapped up at the open and sold off in the first hour. Micron down 5.90 percent, HIVE down 5.67, CleanSpark down 5.36, SanDisk down 5.09, Core Scientific down 5.00.

2. Amazon's headline beat looks worse the further into the cash flow statement you read. The operating quarter is real and I said so this morning. AWS did $42.2 billion, up 37 percent, its fastest in eighteen quarters, at a 39 percent operating margin, adding $4.6 billion of quarterly revenue at roughly 80 percent above its previous record.

Now the forensics. Per a line-item read of the filing, stripping the Anthropic mark leaves earnings of $1.68 per share against guidance of $1.82, a miss and flat year over year. The reported 33 percent rise in operating cash flow rests on an income tax deferral. Undo it and operating cash flow is roughly $27.7 billion, down about 15 percent.

The finding worth carrying forward is the securities line. Purchases of non-marketable securities, meaning stakes in private companies and mostly Anthropic, ran $24.4 billion in the second quarter alone and $39.8 billion across the first half. The comparable figure for all of 2025 was $4.8 billion. Set that against the $25 billion AWS-AI annual run rate Amazon discloses and the circularity is not an accusation, it is arithmetic. The last bond offering was 1.4 times oversubscribed against a typical investment-grade four times, and long-term debt has risen 96 percent in two quarters to $129 billion.

Apple went the other way on the same day for the opposite reason, down about 10 percent on a September guide of 9 to 11 percent against 12 percent expected, blamed explicitly on the memory shortage.

3. Chanos has the best mechanism anyone has offered for why profits look this good. Per Prof G Markets, Jim Chanos makes an accounting-identity argument rather than a valuation one. Capex spenders capitalise and depreciate over five to ten years. The receivers, Nvidia and Caterpillar and the utilities, book revenue and profit immediately. The same dollar therefore inflates aggregate profits, and he sizes the mismatch in the hundreds of billions a year. His arithmetic is that S&P profits should be growing 8 or 9 percent and are growing 28 or 29.

His precedent has teeth. S&P earnings grew 30 percent from mid-1998 to mid-2000, then fell 40 percent from mid-2000 to mid-2001, as much as during the financial crisis, on a GDP decline of only 1 percent. The cause was order-book cancellation. "People realized they didn't need 10,000 routers, they needed 2,000."

He says this is 1999 rather than 1997, and his tell is equity issuance, which he calls foolproof and which did not accelerate last time until late 1999. He also puts hyperscaler return on incremental invested capital as roughly halved in eighteen months, from 100 percent to 25 at some names and the teens at a couple, and gives it twelve to eighteen months before boards start asking. He is short bitcoin miners turned data centre companies, the neoclouds, and Tesla and SpaceX by name, while long the indices including Nvidia.

The honest counterweight comes from Phil Rosen, who has the equal-weight S&P up 12.2 percent this year against 8.7 for the cap-weighted index, the NYSE advance-decline line near all-time highs, two thirds of the index above where it started June and eight of eleven sectors higher. Both men are looking at the same equal-weight strength. Rosen calls it healthy broadening. Chanos calls it proof the whole market is dearer than 1999, when at least Walmart and Caterpillar traded at 10 to 12 times rather than 40 to 50.

I lean Chanos on the mechanism and Rosen on the timing. The profit-inflation argument is arithmetic and does not need a catalyst. The breadth argument says the unwind has not started. Those are compatible, and together they argue the risk is 2027 rather than August.

4. Microsoft changed two accounting policies on the same call, and both flatter the same stock. Per a reading of the transcript, the company said it is "extending the estimated useful life of our data centers and office buildings from 15 to 25 years" at the start of fiscal 2027. That lowers depreciation, which lifts operating profit and margin.

The second change is larger and got almost no coverage. In Microsoft's own words, "more of our future data center leases will shift from finance leases to operating leases as a result of this update. Finance leases are included in capital expenditures while operating leases are not." Reported free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capex, so moving lease spend out of capex mechanically raises it.

Take them together and the profit multiple and the cash flow multiple both improve without the business changing. The stock rose 15 percent on the print. This is checkable against the filing and it silently reprices every model anyone builds on Microsoft next year, including the reverse discounted cash flow work below.

Two of those are worth the space. Per one model, Microsoft at $451.10 implies about 20.3 percent annual unlevered free cash flow growth for a decade against a realised roughly 4 percent over fiscal 2022 to 2025, with a bear case of $202, a base of $310 and a bull of $368. The price sits 23 percent above the author's own bull case. Per the companion, ASML at $1,655 implies 25.5 percent for ten years, taking free cash flow from around $10 billion to roughly $97 billion, against a bull case of $1,086.
