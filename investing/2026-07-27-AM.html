1. Alphabet's obligation wall is the story, and it dwarfs the paper-gains argument. Per Ticker Symbol: YOU, the $99 billion of gains on the Anthropic and SpaceX stakes and the resulting $2.85 of operational earnings per share are both confirmed, so Sunday's read stands. But the same filing shows $811 billion of future purchase agreements against $332 billion three months earlier, about $200 billion of it due within a year, with some energy contracts running to 2054.

The balance sheet agrees. Free cash flow was negative $5.9 billion against positive $24.6 billion two quarters ago, long-term debt more than doubled in six months, the interest bill roughly quintupled, and buybacks have stopped for the first time since 2017.

One correction from Sunday. I gave the cloud backlog as $106 billion to $514 billion over twelve months and this source gives $462 billion to $514 billion. Both are right, because it is one sequence: $106 billion a year ago, $462 billion last quarter, $514 billion now, up more than $50 billion in a single quarter per the Crypto list. The slowing rate of increase is the part nobody is saying out loud.

Two more specifics. The SpaceX stake is marked at $94 billion as of June 30, but $80 billion unlocks in stages to December 8, $14 billion stays frozen into next year's third quarter, and SpaceX is down about 25 percent in a month. That mark is stale in the wrong direction. And for the first time cloud revenue comes primarily from selling TPU systems rather than renting them, which neither Microsoft nor Amazon does.

Alex at Ticker Symbol: YOU says Wall Street is making a big mistake and is buying. Per Stocks with Josh, Google's MACD and signal line both sit beneath the zero line and his instruction is to leave it alone until they clear it. My lean is the momentum read for two weeks and the fundamental read for two years, because a $200 billion obligation inside twelve months reprices a stock rather than merely worrying it.

2. Three venues are publishing three different numbers on the same Fed decision, and that gap is the trade. Per the Kalshi list, Kalshi has an 83 percent chance the Fed holds in July. That is notable on its own, because Kalshi published no rate market at all three days before the last decision. Per the Financial Markets list, Barchart says hike odds have climbed to 36 percent, while Evan at StockMKTNewz puts Polymarket traders at 17 percent on a hike. Those cannot all be right.

Charlie Bilello supplies the frame: the bond market priced two cuts in January and now prices one to two hikes, a full one percent swing. October hike odds have passed 75 percent per the Stocks list.

So take the lean. A hold is 85/15, unchanged from Sunday. But the sharper trade is not the decision. Per the Stocks list, MoneyPrinter0x argues the edge is that forward-looking hike sentiment has topped and mean-reverting down would lift rate-sensitive risk assets, and Citi is calling for a dovish outcome. I put a hawkish-enough statement to move the tape at 45/55, down from 50/50 on Sunday, because oil falling 7 percent overnight removes the one input that was forcing Warsh's hand.

3. CXMT is the memory trade's new variable and the loudest bull is telling you why it does not matter yet. Per the Crypto list, Melvin calls it the largest chip IPO in mainland China's history. China's biggest DRAM maker has gone from under 4 percent to roughly 7.7 percent of global DRAM in under a year, first-quarter revenue up 719 percent to 50.8 billion yuan. On the raise, he says roughly $9.8 billion while the Financial Markets list reports $8.6 billion, and that is not a rounding difference.

His bear case on his own bull day is the useful part. CXMT's monthly wafer output is capped near 290,000 to 320,000 against Samsung's roughly 630,000 and SK Hynix's near 500,000, and its yields run about 42 percent below their mature-process yields, so on HBM "it won't compete there for years." The shortage driving DRAM and NAND up 60 percent or more per quarter does not end because a new Chinese supplier showed up.

Two desks read the identical fact opposite ways. Per the Financial Markets list, KraneShares argues US export restrictions were "designed as a ceiling" and have "become a floor," while zerohedge calls the same facts China's two-front squeeze on America's AI build-out. Both describe a Chinese supplier that got stronger. Only one thinks that is America's problem.

4. Nvidia is now underwriting its own customer's debt, and the bear argument against it is specific rather than vague. Per the Market list, Nvidia is in talks to guarantee up to $250 billion of financing for a 10-gigawatt OpenAI campus in southern Ohio developed by SoftBank's energy arm, with total project cost possibly above $500 billion, power controlled by the US government and funded by Japan under a recent trade deal, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick involved in allocating it. OpenAI has no investment-grade rating, so the guarantee is what makes the debt cheap. Separately, Nvidia is discussing financing chip purchases that could total $350 billion.

Per Jose Najarro, the correction that matters is that a $250 billion guarantee is not $250 billion of funding, and Nvidia pays only if OpenAI misses. His bull read is that prior backstops ran $3 billion to $6 billion, so a number this size is existential and nobody fakes an existential commitment. His open question is whether Nvidia keeps and re-rents the campus on a default.

The bear case, from Douglas Swift on the Stocks list, is the sentence to sit with: a vendor is backstopping its customer's balance sheet to secure its own order book, the structure by which Lucent and Nortel financed the CLECs that bought their switches, and "when the supplier underwrites the demand, the demand signal stops being independent information." Wayne Liang asks why Nvidia would underwrite $250 billion of demand that supposedly is not real. Swift just answered him. And the pattern runs wide: $1 billion into Nokia, $2 billion into Marvell, $1 billion into Naver, equity in Lumentum and Coherent, $500 billion-plus with SK Group, $30 billion already in OpenAI.

5. Supply got locked up in size over the weekend, and both companies doing it report inside 48 hours. Per Najarro, SK Group and Nvidia signed above $500 billion covering next-generation AI memory including HBM, with SK Hynix also building its own AI cloud business on Nvidia's platform, first site online in 2027. Samsung and Broadcom signed roughly $200 billion across memory and foundry over five years, and the foundry leg is the interesting half, because Broadcom becomes a 2-nanometer customer as a condition of getting the memory. Locking in supply has become the moat.

Larry Fink said it plainly on the People to follow list: "We have more demand for memory than we have supply," and maybe in three or four years there is enough. Against that, Charlie Bilello has the discipline datapoint of the week on the same list. Apple generated $129 billion of free cash flow last year and Oracle burned $24 billion, and the stocks went plus 56 and minus 52 percent. Per Chip Stock Investor, the tape is wobbling because markets price capex six to twelve months ahead and the visibility window that lengthened in the first half is now contracting.
