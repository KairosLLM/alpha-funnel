1. Korea halted and the memory bull case got stronger, and that pairing is the whole story. Per the Crypto list, Melvin's supply data is the densest thing on the tape. TrendForce has DRAM contract prices up 90 to 95 percent quarter over quarter in the first quarter and NAND up 55 to 60, with another 58 to 63 percent DRAM jump in the second.

Samsung memory average selling prices are up 146 percent against the 2025 average and it hiked HBM3E another 20 percent for 2026 orders before HBM4 ramps. SK Hynix says HBM, DRAM and NAND are essentially sold out for the year, Micron has left consumer memory entirely, and new capacity does not arrive meaningfully until late 2027 because HBM eats roughly three times the wafer capacity per gigabyte of standard DRAM.

Samsung also forecast a nineteen-fold jump in quarterly operating profit while its stock was falling, and it and SK Hynix have committed $520 billion to four new plants. Korea broke anyway on index construction plus leverage: the two names are roughly half the KOSPI, and the selling concentrated in leveraged single-stock products tracking exactly those two.

So take the lean. The supply story is intact at 75/25, up from 70/25 last night, because every datapoint this morning tightened it. But John Galt's bear case deserves respect, and per the Stocks list it is specific: a decisive breakdown in Hynix pointing at 1 to 1.1 million won, another 35 to 40 percent lower, consistent with $SMH in the 335 to 400 range over several months. Note also that Jordi Visser has trimmed memory, because the DRAM ETF took six billion dollars in one of the fastest inflows in history and retail is heavily involved. A right thesis bought at a crowded entry is still a bad entry.

2. The actual break is in credit, and it set a record last night. Per the Financial Markets list, zerohedge reports "unprecedented carnage in AI IG CDS" with the hyperscaler CDS basket another 3bps wider to a record. Per the Market list, the cost of insuring Nvidia's debt "surged to a record" after reports it is discussing more than $750 billion of AI infrastructure financing tied to OpenAI. I have been carrying $250 billion for the Ohio campus, and the reported total is now triple that.

Charlie Bilello supplies the rates half: the 30-year ended last week at 5.17 percent, its highest weekly close since July 2007. The financing machine did not slow. BlackRock closed a $12.5 billion bond sale against a Meta data centre in Texas, the 2048 notes priced 287.5 basis points over ten-year Treasuries with roughly $20 billion of demand, about 1.6 times the deal, BlackRock-backed entities owning 80 percent and Meta 20, backed by lease commitments so the debt stays off Meta's balance sheet.

Jim Cramer named the precedent: "I lived through 2000. I don't want the sequel," and "What we learned in 2000 is that you don't lend to companies who buy your goods." His conditional is the honest version: if OpenAI can pay, perhaps because it comes public, Nvidia is fine, and if not, different story. He also told anyone who borrowed to buy the data-centre theme to sell at today's open, while calling the Nvidia selloff "absurd" and saying he still owns it. Same session.

Goldman's desk put numbers on the internals: momentum factor down 7 percent, software and internet up 4 against semis down 3.5, AMD down 6, SanDisk down 13, semicap down 5 to 7, on three named catalysts. The Nvidia-OpenAI deal reigniting the circular-financing debate, China starting homegrown DUV tools, and cheaper Chinese open models.

3. The best single explanation of why this is happening came from a reader's saved post rather than a bank. Per Alex Corrino, the proximate cause is real rates, and his evidence starts with a date: "the Nasdaq topped the day of Warsh's first FOMC meeting." Long-run inflation expectations have barely moved while Treasury yields have, worth roughly 50 basis points of higher real rates. Warsh drives both legs, because the committee has begun indicating hikes and he is an avowed balance-sheet hawk who plans to let mortgage-backed securities and long-duration Treasuries roll off, removing a buyer of duration.

The collision is with peak capex. The AI buildout is one of the longest-duration trades in the market, so higher real rates hit the present value of data-centre cash flows hardest, and the cushion that would have made debt unnecessary is already spent. His scoreboard: Amazon and Alphabet flat on the year, Meta down 10 percent and Microsoft down 20, against a Nasdaq 100 up 10.

His forward call is a bet against the sell side: "analysts haven't begun to move semis EPS expects down yet," and the market may be sniffing it out before they do. He then does what most macro writers will not and concedes the trade does not work, because hyperscalers treat AI as existential and semis have verifiably high backlogs while staying exposed. Read it as a frame rather than a position.

4. The money went into industrials, and two desks found it independently on the same morning. Per BowTied Bull, whose last item was a 69-word paywalled stub and who this time published in full, the Nasdaq topped around June while the Russell 2000 is up since June. He eliminates financials, because a tech seller is not buying banks, and energy, because a tech mandate cannot hold it, then lands on industrials. His named holdings there: Caterpillar, GE Vernova, GE Aerospace, RTX, Eaton and Boeing. The useful part is his framing, that "you can argue that industrials are technology in heavy machinery form," an easier story to tell a committee than oil.

Yardeni Research reached the same place hours later: "Investors can't figure out AI's future returns, but Industrials are outperforming Tech this year. The capex is real even if the payoff isn't clear yet."

Two caveats. His financials logic is contradicted by the tape, because JPMorgan hit a record high in the same session on its way to a $937 billion market cap. And there is a competing answer, because per the People to follow list capital also rotated into crypto equities, with BitMine up 13.49 percent at $17.96 after adding nearly 10,000 ETH. Both can be partly right, and neither is measured.

5. Four institutions are on three different sides of tomorrow, and the venues cannot agree either. Per the Kalshi list, its traders forecast 73 percent that the Fed holds, and separately 69 percent for a hike this year against 19 for a cut. That leaves roughly 12 percent for no change all year, which does not sit beside 73 percent hold on one meeting.

Polymarket has 73 percent no change and 26 percent for a quarter-point hike, with a full-year distribution of two hikes at 32, one at 31, none at 29. Citadel Securities expects a hike, Citigroup expects a hold, and Melvin says futures price basically zero chance of a surprise and "this is a hold meeting, not a hawkish pivot." Ben Emons of FedWatch Advisors gives the hawks their mechanism: "Within the Fed there is a big shift of tone. People are eager to start hiking again because they worry about inflation going higher."

My numbers move again. A hold goes to 75/25 from 80/20, because two named institutions now expect a hike and the front-end repricing has run one way for a week. A hawkish-enough statement stays at 60/40. If Corrino is right that Warsh wants forward guidance out of his mandate, the statement is where the damage lands.
