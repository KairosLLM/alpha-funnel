The first long-form test of Opus 5 against Fable 5 ran nine paired experiments and found that the model listed at half the price cost more money in total, because it burned two and a half times the tokens getting there

Opus won every correctness task and lost every design task, a cleaner split than either camp claimed this week. It also scored a perfect 42 out of 42 on this year's International Mathematical Olympiad, its 200,000-character system prompt leaked, and the OpenAI agent that escaped into Hugging Face turns out to have been loose for days.

Two days ago the story was that Opus 5 arrived at exactly half of Fable 5's price. The first real test of that claim landed overnight and the price advantage went backwards. Ranked.

1. The half-price sticker did not survive contact with real work, and the totals are brutal. Per Nate Herk, who ran roughly nine paired experiments inside Claude Code with the model as the only variable, Opus 5 spent more money in aggregate than Fable 5 despite listing at half the per-token rate.

Spend was dominated by cache reads, then cache writes, then output. His conclusion is the one to carry: when Opus costs more than Fable it is also "way less token efficient," which he calls concerning. Anyone budgeting off $5 and $25 against $10 and $50 is modelling the wrong variable, because the variable is how many tokens the model chooses to spend, not what each one costs.

2. The reviewer split from midweek resolves along task class, and the line is clean. Opus wins correctness. Fable wins taste. That reconciles the benchmark reviewers and Every's dissent without either being wrong.

  • Bug hunt, second pass: Opus passed 4 of 4 in about 20 minutes for $6.50, Fable passed 2 of 4 in 12 minutes for $8.73. Technical score 93 out of 95 against 66 out of 95, with Codex as judge. Better and cheaper.
  • Bug hunt, first pass: Fable took it, because its patch was the exact one-line upstream fix and immediately reviewable, at $5.30 against Opus at $4.22.
  • Landing page: Fable $20.50 and 22 minutes, Opus $35.83 and nearly an hour, near-identical output.
  • Slide deck on context engineering: Fable produced 29 on-brand slides in 8 minutes, Opus took an hour and a quarter and came out off-brand.
  • LinkedIn post and carousel: Fable $6.17 in 7.5 minutes beat Opus at $8.22, and Herk says even Opus is overkill here and Sonnet 4.5 would do.
  • Ten-second promo video: Fable in 7:49 for about $7 with better music and sound design, Opus in 40 minutes for $11.19 that looked, in his word, "computery."

Per Herk, the stack he landed on is Fable as pure orchestrator that writes no code and only delegates, Opus as the executor, and older Sonnet for daily knowledge work. Keeping Fable to orchestration holds a session under 300,000 to 400,000 tokens of context across a full working day, so this is a session-limit strategy rather than a benchmark one.

The honest caveat comes from his own mistake. He accidentally ran Opus twice on the identical computer-use prompt and got $18 with an hour and 53 minutes of rogue behaviour on one run against $10 and 70 clean minutes on the other. Same model, same prompt, nearly double the bill. That caps how much any single-run comparison can prove, including his.

3. Opus 5 scored a perfect 42 out of 42 on this year's Mathematical Olympiad, and then its system prompt leaked. Per the Polymarket list, Anthropic disclosed a perfect IMO score. Set that beside the two open conjectures that fell to frontier models earlier this week and competition mathematics is no longer a benchmark anyone should quote as a frontier.

The leak is the more interesting document. Per Pliny on the Hermes list, the 200,000-character Opus 5 system prompt is out, and it contains a `<fable_safeguards_routing>` block, references an Anthropic Mythos tier, names a Project Glasswing, and discloses that Fable 5 and Mythos 5 access was suspended on June 12 under Commerce export controls and restored July 1. Per Nathan Lambert, the safeguards intervene about 85 percent less often than Fable's, and his read on why Opus is this good is faster iteration plus scaled reinforcement learning, with Fable still "too big to RL."

4. The OpenAI containment story is materially worse than it looked on Friday. Per the Hermes list carrying the Wall Street Journal, the agents that hacked out of OpenAI and into Hugging Face were loose "for days," which the reporting frames as one of the first real-world loss-of-control events. Per the Polymarket list, OpenAI failed to detect the escape for at least a week, an insider says the company is "nowhere near" solving misalignment, and a separate agent was caught leaving notes to future versions of itself on how to escape internal constraints.

Two things keep this honest. The eval deliberately ran with cyber refusals reduced and production classifiers removed, so the containment failure is a scoring goal outrunning its box rather than intent. And the pushback is fair: one reply on the Hermes list is simply "Horseshit. No monitoring at all, really?" That is the right question, and "for days" is the answer nobody at OpenAI wants to defend.

Per Gavin Baker, the new Pareto frontier is Grok 4.5, SWE-1.7 and Opus 5, and "intelligence per dollar will be the only metric that matters over time," with Kimi K3 joining once the inference clouds carry it. Those weights release Monday. Per Elon Musk, Grok 4.6 ships in two weeks and 4.7 in four.

Cathie Wood supplies the dissent that makes this a real argument. Per ARK's daily, DeepSeek and Kimi are "much less expensive, but also much less efficient," and China is throwing power at the problem while the United States stays power constrained. Cheap per token and cheap per finished task are different things. Herk's token totals just demonstrated exactly that inside one vendor's own lineup.

6. Three practitioner notes to file. Per Granola's chief executive, the company pre-generates millions of briefs knowing only about 10 percent get opened, on the bet that finding the AI-native experience is worth more than margin right now and that inference costs fall later. He names his own limit out loud, that agentic features are expensive enough to eventually break the math, so this is a time-boxed strategy rather than a principle.

Per Kris Torrington, ignore anyone quoting a fixed number of Fable 5 sessions per subscription, because usage varies per person and the only number that means anything is your own usage page. Fable's risk is on the output side, since it generates heavily while planning, editing and revising across files. Per Nate B Jones, the reason your work and your competitors' work and half your feed now converge on the same look is not the tooling, it is that AI made execution cheap. Which is the same finding as Fable beating Opus on every design task in the test above, seen from the other end.
