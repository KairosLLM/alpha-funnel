Jensen Huang's first post in the history of his X account was to sign the open-weights letter, and Anthropic's silence is now priced at half a percent

Polymarket has the company signing at 0.5 percent against 99.5 percent no. Meanwhile a named former Anthropic employee has made the sharpest allegation of the whole fight, that sales staff used committed-spend contracts as a precondition for lowering safeguards, and Anthropic has answered none of it.

Four days into Anthropic standing alone, the argument has stopped being about policy and become about conduct. Ranked.

1. The letter hardened into two camps and the biggest name in hardware joined loudly. Per the Hermes list, Jensen Huang's first post ever on X was to sign it: "AI will transform every industry, power every company, and be built by every country. Open models strengthen safety and cybersecurity, accelerate innovation and diffusion, and enable sovereignty. The world needs both frontier closed models and frontier open models." Satya Nadella endorsed the same dual-ecosystem framing, and Microsoft, Meta, Palantir and IBM sit on that side. Anthropic's position is now a traded instrument, with Polymarket pricing it signing at 0.5 percent against 99.5 no.

The best defence of Anthropic came from someone with no stake in defending it. Per the same digest, signulll argues that if an open-weight model is ever involved in a serious safety incident, "anthropic's position will look a lot more prescient than many of its critics are willing to admit today," and that this is what a principled stance costs. The sharpest attack on the reasoning came from mike64_t, who calls the absolutist framing a literal Pascal's wager and says "the low resource bad actor magically gated by access to a model is a fiction of the EA mind that doesn't exist." His counter-proposal is concrete: if the labs care about software safety, fund continuous fuzzing at the Linux Foundation and the FSF.

2. Two unanswered questions about Anthropic's conduct landed in the same weekend. Handle the first carefully, because it is an allegation, not a finding. Per the Hermes list, Noah Lebovic, who says he used to work at Anthropic, makes three claims at once. That the capability argument is moot, because he used Opus 4.6 in February to reach other people's medical records and hijack bank accounts, and GLM 5.1 outperforms it in most pentesting environments. That misuse flows through closed models anyway, since "the sketchier folks I know are still using a Claude Code or Codex subscription for hacking."

And the one that matters: "I know of two instances where two different Anthropic GTM people used large comitted spend contracts as a prereq for lowering safeguards, and I directly witnessed one."

That is unverified, uncorroborated and carries no response from Anthropic in any source this morning. I am printing it because a named former insider alleging that safety thresholds were negotiable against contract size goes straight to the credibility of the company's public argument, and because it is falsifiable in a way most of this week's commentary is not.

The second question is about published artifacts. Per the same digest, om_patel5 says every app, doc, dashboard and tool published from Claude is indexed and searchable, and that what has already surfaced includes internal dashboards, client project plans, a clinical trial summary with real medical data, cap tables and payroll with employee names.

The design critique is the fair one, because publishing offers only "only me" or "anyone with the link," and as he puts it, "nobody reading that thinks it means 'and google will list it publicly.'" Third-party sites have begun scraping these into collections, so removing a page from search no longer removes the content. Single account, no outlet confirmation, no Anthropic response.

One piece of evidence cuts against the closed-is-safer premise. Per the same digest, a Wall Street Journal investigation found that after OpenAI raised model capability last summer, hundreds of users asked ChatGPT for step-by-step instructions on producing biological weapons and poisons, with employees calling the outputs simple enough for a high-school biology student. OpenAI banned the accounts and did not notify law enforcement.

3. Local inference now has its electricity bill written out, and an eight-gigabyte card built a working engine. Per the Hermes list, sudoingX measured bonsai 27b, a one-bit crush of Qwen 3.6 27B to 3.9GB.

His headline claim: 42 tokens a second at 160 watts is about seven million output tokens for a dollar of electricity, against roughly a hundred thousand for that dollar at ten dollars per million on a frontier API. Seventy times the output on a two-hundred-dollar card. The thirty-five watts between those cards is the whole difference between a machine that chats and one that runs an agent loop at full context.

Given one spec, bonsai also produced a working rotary engine simulation autonomously, an epitrochoid housing with a triangular rotor turning at a third of shaft speed, passing ten geometry tests, and he volunteers that it broke three times getting there. Two caveats stay attached. Every figure is self-measured with no third-party verification, and he is openly building a show around these runs.

4. Kimi K3 is on a public countdown, Meta says it will ship open weights, and the ban everyone is arguing about is not coming. Per the Hermes list, Moonshot has a live countdown on the Hugging Face repo for K3. That is a real advance on three days ago, when the only signal was a prediction market on timing, and it is still not a release. No weights, no date, no benchmark, no parameter count. Alexandr Wang says Meta will launch a harness and open-source models, a single-sourced quote with no name, license or date attached.

The policy half is quieter than the rhetoric. Per threadguy, a ban on Chinese models is not happening: "I don't think there's any chance that's happening," and it would not be enforceable anyway because "we will just fly to China and come back with some flash drives." He reframes the real question as government procurement rather than prohibition, and that is the more useful frame. It is one person's read on administration sentiment with no bill or agency named.

5. The guidance for writing agent instructions just inverted, and the judgment that survives it is about money. Per the Hermes list, Anthropic cut the Claude Code system prompt by roughly 80 percent for its newest model line, with Tariq Shihipar saying the newer models want a smaller system prompt and that long examples and hard constraints now limit rather than improve performance. Hard rules like "never write comments" gave way to goal-oriented guidance and skills loaded on demand, and a new `/doctor` command audits instruction files for rules written for older generations. A large hand-tuned instruction file is now a liability rather than an asset.

Per Kun Chen, a 101-minute session shipped a genuinely working full-stack app end to end, native SwiftUI on a Fastify and Postgres backend, provisioned on a VPS through infrastructure as code, finishing with a real Apple sign-in from the simulator and a deposit that persisted across a sign-out. He deliberately ran a cheaper model at high reasoning effort to make the point, and his own summary is the finding: the only place it was not smart enough was the hosting cost, where it estimated $30 to $60 a month for something that cost $5.50. A cheap model executed the build correctly and got exactly one thing wrong, and it was the money. He also names the failure mode, that GPT 5.6 over-engineers, and he killed three separate instances of it.

Per Kris Torrington, the rubric version is five habits, the brief, the checkpoint, the evidence, the correction and the permission level, under an earned-autonomy rule: expand authority only after repeated evidence-backed success at the current level. His best line is that "a surprising amount of agent unreliability starts as weak management wearing a technical costume." Be clear what that source is, an explainer built on Anthropic's own documentation with no original testing and no benchmark. Useful as a checklist only.

And per Nate B Jones, the big five hyperscalers are on track to spend north of $600 billion on capex this year, up roughly a third. He argues the industry has stopped competing on who has the best model, then the clip ends before he says what replaced it. Take the $600 billion and leave the thesis.

6. A note on attribution, because today's clip genuinely cannot carry one. Saturday I corrected a misattribution on this publisher and Sunday a follow-up clip confirmed the correction. Today's clip from the same source argues that agents and users should share one interface rather than talk through a protocol: give the agent a CLI and the user a web view inside the agent's in-app browser, then let the agent choose, with the catch that the CLI has to modify the state of the UI.

Per the clip itself, there is exactly one speaker marker in it, it lands mid-sentence, and there is no first-person product claim anywhere. No "our app," no company, no product of the speaker's own. So unlike Sunday's clip, which identified its speaker twice through a possessive, this one identifies nobody at all. The honest attribution is a speaker on an Every podcast, and the title names the episode's guest rather than the person talking. Same trap, one step on, and the right answer is to name no one.
