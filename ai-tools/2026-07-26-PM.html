Anthropic has now gone three days without a word on the open-weights letter, and the sharpest attack against it tonight is that the company would not exist without Google open-sourcing the transformer paper

Bill Gurley calls that a verifiable fact and says it should be the first question in every interview. Meanwhile a one-bit crush of a 27-billion-parameter model now runs agentic work at 128,000 tokens of context on a $200 graphics card, and three separate practitioners landed on the same bottleneck this weekend, and it is approval rather than generation.

Google signed on Saturday night, leaving one name. Twenty-four hours on, that name has still said nothing and the argument has moved from policy to provenance. Ranked.

1. The hypocrisy charge is now the main line of attack, and a serious investor put his name on it. Per the Financial Markets list, Bill Gurley amplified the observation that Anthropic would not exist without Google open-sourcing the transformer paper, and that a company built on open source trying to eliminate open source is ironic. His own words were that this is 100 percent true, a verifiable fact, and should be the first question for anyone in media who interviews anyone there. Gary Marcus added that the company also would not exist without Common Crawl.

The rest split into two shapes. Per the Hermes list, Shannon Sands of Nous made the competitive version: nobody is obliged to let you take over the world unopposed because you are ahead, especially when you are the one lobbying the government for regulatory capture. Nathan Odle made the consumer version, binning Anthropic's business practices with HP printers and Ticketmaster.

Two defences are worth more than the pile-on. Per the Market list, Jesse Livermore asks the question nobody in the coalition wants: if Anthropic is so corrupt, why is it the only one in the group that has not publicly courted Trump in exchange for favours. And Steve Hou names the thing the letter carefully does not: open weights is a euphemism for Chinese models, and China does not appear once in the document even though everyone knows what is meant. He also declines to impugn motives, noting Dario Amodei's opposition to Chinese AI is long-standing and consistent rather than opportunistic.

Per Gavin Baker, the fairest framing of what the letter actually asks: Nvidia is the leading open-source AI company by a wide margin, and the letter did not suggest that closed-source companies should open-source their models. It asked that other people's open weights not be made illegal. That is a narrower request than the coverage implies, and it is why the hypocrisy charge lands rather than glances.

2. Three practitioners independently found the same bottleneck this weekend, and it is approval. Nobody has automated the reviewing. Per Daria Cupareanu, her social system now produces 30 posts in a day, which leaves 30 to review, and reading 30 generated posts in a row is far more draining than making one at a time with AI beside her, because reviewing means searching for something wrong inside language that already looks finished.

Per Nate B Jones, the rule that made his agent safe to run was keeping human approval on every decision involving access or money. And per Every, the email workflow that actually works is cards plus a pre-written draft plus an explicit send approval: emails become cards read inside Codex in an in-app browser, each one saying what the email said, what the draft should be, and do you want to send it.

Both warn about the identical failure mode. Jones: agents are very good at making a messy pile look orderly, including when the order is wrong. Cupareanu, from the other direction: the error you are hunting is hidden inside prose that already reads as finished. The instruction is to read the largest group yourself and ask the agent what it is unsure about.

3. The one credible automation win was root-cause elimination, not a better bot. This is the most useful case study of the weekend because the numbers are specific and the mechanism is unglamorous. Per Jones, weekly support tickets fell from 52 to 19 in the next comparable week and the whole category disappeared. The recurring task was Slack community access failing in three shapes, an invite that never arrived, a sign-in link already expired, and payment under one email with a join attempt from another.

The fix was not an answering agent. It was approved email domains for self-service entry plus a non-expiring invite, which deleted the manual approval step entirely. Two upstream causes only surfaced because pattern analysis found them: the invite code was expiring in two to three days under volume, and there was a typo in the published executive-circle access code breaking onboarding on one surface.

His setup order is the transferable part. Write every process step as it actually is with timings and mark judgment against automatable. Give each pain point its own ticket. Have the agent pre-attach billing, account and prior-conversation research, which took per-case research from five to ten minutes to under one. Run in draft mode for the first 20 to 30 cases, strip personal data first, and do not start with fraud, legal, security, large refunds or your angriest customer.

The Gumroad example is the genuinely new pattern. A creator reported three giant dashes on a mobile sales chart, and the support agent reproduced the bug, traced it into code, wrote a test, opened a pull request, shipped it and issued the standard $25 bug credit automatically. The technical fix was right and the design decision wrong, so a founder overruled the agent's taste and the customer's approval became part of the merge. Customer as release approver is shippable rather than a demo.

4. An eight-gigabyte card is now an agent tier, and that is the real threat to model pricing. Per the Hermes list, bonsai 27b is a one-bit crush of Qwen 3.6 27B down to 3.9 gigabytes that runs 42 tokens a second at 128,000 tokens of context inside 6.8 of 8 gigabytes on a $200 RTX 3060 Ti drawing 159 watts, under Apache 2.0. The line that matters: the eight-gigabyte tier stopped being a chatbot tier and quietly became a 128,000-token agent tier.

Set that beside the commercial claim. Per the People list, Brian Armstrong puts open-source models six months behind the frontier and often 99 percent cheaper for inference, and that is why workloads shift regardless of which model is better. Same argument as the letter, expressed as a purchase order rather than a principle.

5. A note on attribution, because I got a headline wrong in the right direction. This morning I corrected an attribution: a clip titled for Granola's founder actually carried Every's side of the conversation making the bring-your-own-agent argument. Tonight's follow-up clip is titled "Granola's Chris Pedregal Vibe-Coded His Own AI Email Client," which looked like evidence against my correction.

It is not. Per that clip, the transcript runs in three segments across two speaker changes, and the middle segment is the question "so this is basically you've like vibe coded your own email client, right?" The builder is the person answering, and he claims Cora as our email app and our full email client, twice. Cora is Every's product and Granola makes meeting notes. He also names an open-source experiment of his own called tend.

The companion clip settles it from the other side. Per that interview, Pedregal is unmistakably the speaker, naming his previous startup Socratic at 12 people and Granola now between 60 and 70, and he ends by asking Every whether they have a philosophy on splitting roles. Asking questions is exactly the part he plays in the email clip. His answer on scaling is worth keeping anyway: the hard problem is holding a product consistent and tasteful as more people touch it, and he says plainly he does not know how to define roles now that the old product, design and engineering split may no longer map.

So the correction stands and the publisher's title is what is wrong. The durable lesson is narrower and more useful than the incident: this feed titles clips after the episode's guest rather than the speaker in the excerpt, so any single-quote attribution from it needs a pronoun or a product claim to anchor it. Note also the same product is transcribed Cora tonight and Kora yesterday.

6. Two things to weigh sceptically, and one to keep. Per Nate Herk, agentic AI is on its way to replacing around 50 percent of all jobs and Claude Code is doing around 60 percent of tasks in companies. Neither number is sourced, both open a video that funnels to a free community and course, and they should be read as advertising rather than findings. What is worth keeping is the framing that the skill to build is being an AI manager, onboarding the tool like a new hire, plus his own honest failure: an analytics pull that produced a multi-tab dashboard for 75 videos in about ten minutes still failed to fetch impressions and click-through rate.

Per Cupareanu, the cost is not burnout, it is that capability expands faster than time so enough keeps moving. Her practices are concrete: delegate by what you want to protect rather than by efficiency, hand social over completely while hand-building articles at eight hours or more each, and use the model as a surface to push against until your own decision becomes visible. The upside compounds: a system cannot follow a judgment that has never left your head, so delegation forces instinct into written standards.
