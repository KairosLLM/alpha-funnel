A Chinese lab valued at a thirtieth of OpenAI shipped a model that matches the frontier at a third of the price, and by Saturday night it had turned the American AI industry against itself with Anthropic standing alone

Kimi K3's weights land on Hugging Face this morning. In seven days the response went from a lobbying memo about manufacturing regulatory uncertainty, through a White House accusation that Moonshot distilled Fable 5 on Nvidia chips in Thailand, to Nvidia's open-weights letter being signed by OpenAI and then Google. The fight is no longer about China.

The weights drop this morning, so the week to understand is the one that just happened around them. A model from a company worth roughly a thirtieth of its American rivals forced every lab, the White House, the Treasury and eventually Jensen Huang's first-ever post on X to take a public position. Here is the arc, the economics, the policy fight, the technical substance, and what to watch.

The arc of the week. It started with a neutral scorecard, which mattered because the first wave of Kimi coverage had been vendor-flavoured on both sides. Vals AI ran 38 models and put Fable 5 at 75.14 percent, Kimi K3 second at 74.7, and GPT-5.6 Sol at 73.12, with K3 hitting 95.1 percent on their SWE-bench Verified subset and 1,679 Elo on the front-end arena ahead of both. It placed only fourth on the Artificial Analysis intelligence index at 57, and that split between task benchmarks and aggregate indices ran all week.

Monday the political framing arrived before the commercial one. Per David Ondrej, K3 was already the biggest moment in open-source AI, bigger than the DeepSeek shock, with weights mirrored beyond Hugging Face and the line that settles the enforcement question: there is no way to ban math. By evening Moonshot had paused new signups having exhausted its own GPUs, Microsoft was testing K3 for Copilot on Azure, and Xi Jinping had appeared at the Shanghai World AI Conference urging the world to embrace open-source AI while Huawei showed an 8,192-chip cluster with no American silicon in it.

Tuesday the American response became explicit and someone put it in writing. Dean Ball, OpenAI's head of strategic futures, posted a six-point thread reaching over 10 million views arguing the administration should direct every agency to issue soft law manufacturing regulatory uncertainty, and that it "needn't be that well justified." He later said he was forecasting rather than prescribing. David Sacks called the weaponisation of regulatory uncertainty as a competitive tool completely unacceptable and named an OpenAI and Anthropic duopoly. Tyler Cowen said a ban will fail miserably.

Wednesday the state added instruments. Treasury Secretary Bessent said the administration can sanction overseas models that steal American intellectual property and would look for American watermarks inside Chinese models, with an executive order under consideration that would force even routers like OpenRouter to stop serving them. China's commerce ministry reportedly pressed Alibaba, ByteDance and Zhipu to limit overseas weight downloads, the same instinct from the opposite direction.

Thursday brought the accusation. Michael Kratsios, the White House science director, alleged Moonshot distilled Fable 5 into K3 in violation of Anthropic's terms, aggregating and reselling Fable tokens across many accounts to evade detection, and training on Nvidia GB300 servers in Thailand. Bessent weighed sanctions and an Entity List designation. Roughly 200 Silicon Valley firms including Y Combinator signed a letter opposing a ban, while Anthropic's Sarah Heck backed Kratsios and Anthropic doubled its political spending from $20 million to $40 million.

Friday the counter-campaign landed with a signature list, and Saturday it broke open. Per the Hermes list, Jensen Huang opened an X account at 1:18pm purely to post Nvidia's open-weights letter, Satya Nadella circulated it the same morning, Sam Altman replied at 3:55pm. Then at 12:51am Saturday, tae kim reported that OpenAI had signed it, with the line that framed the rest of the weekend: "Your move, Anthropic!" Sundar Pichai signed for Google at 2:39pm citing Gemma. Matthew Berman at 3:11pm: "Only one company left…"

Chamath Palihapitiya's framing is the one that will outlive the week. Against $81 for a barrel of crude, his cost of a barrel of intelligence runs Chinese models at 50 cents, xAI's Grok and Google DeepMind at a dollar, Meta's Llama at $1.50, OpenAI and standard Anthropic at $26, and Anthropic's newest at $56. Per Gavin Baker, the new frontier is Grok 4.5, SWE-1.7 and Opus 5, with K3 joining once the inference clouds carry it, and intelligence per dollar will be the only metric that matters over time.

The supply side of that argument came from DeepSeek's Liang Wenfeng on a four-hour investor call: 85 percent inference margins, around 20,000 Hopper-equivalent chips, a billion dollars of API revenue being enough to be cash-flow positive, and a GPU paying for itself in ten months against a three-to-five-year depreciation schedule. If those numbers are close to true, the American cost structure is a choice rather than a law of physics.

Now the correction that keeps this honest, because cheap per token is not cheap per task. Last Sunday's memo already carried the receipt: Chase AI's build test had K3 at $3 per million input against Fable's $10, and yet roughly $95 per finished task against $14 for GPT-5.6, burning 21.5 million tokens over 90 minutes on work Fable finished in 17.

Nate Herk proved the same thing inside one vendor this week, with Opus 5 burning 2 million output tokens against Fable's 832,000 and spending more in aggregate despite listing at half the price. Cathie Wood's version is blunter: DeepSeek and Kimi are much less expensive and also much less efficient, with China throwing power at the problem while America stays power-constrained. Anyone modelling this off a rate card is modelling the wrong variable.

The policy fight, and why the sides are not what they look like. Nvidia's letter is worth reading for what it concedes rather than what it claims. It argues openness is a safety strategy, admits plainly that released weights are beyond the original developer's control, and spends a full paragraph defending distillation by name as legitimate. That paragraph is not an accident. It was written the same week the White House accused a Chinese lab of distillation, and it exists to pre-empt the legal theory.

Follow the incentives and the coalition makes sense. Per Packy McCormick, this is eight trillion dollars of market capitalisation coming out for open weights, and the frame is Joel Spolsky's old rule that smart companies commoditise their complements. Nvidia sells the chips that run the models, Microsoft sells the products, Palantir wants to sit above the model layer, CrowdStrike needs defenders at parity with attackers, Google gets cheaper serving. Every signatory profits from a world where no lab owns the frontier outright. Bill Gurley put the substance in one line: positioning open models as dumping is misinformed.

Chamath supplied the only argument with a tradeable claim in it, and it points the opposite way from intuition. A ban tanks the market, not debatable, because an ordinary enterprise buyer restricted to two approved vendors pays 50 to 100 times its next-best input and gets rerated while its foreign competitors keep the cheap one. Then the turn: if the government actually bans open source, Anthropic's and OpenAI's own valuations crater, because ban-derived revenue is regulatory capture confined to one market rather than demand. The apparent policy winners are the losers.

Sacks contributed the fact that does the most work. Cursor's Composer 2 was post-trained on Kimi 2.5 using Cursor's own proprietary coding data, producing an American derivative model, and once weights are public it is not a Chinese model any more with no data going back to China. Telling American companies they cannot fork open weights would, in his words, put a dagger through the heart of the entire American open-source ecosystem.

The most striking fact about the weekend is the silence. Anthropic's only voice across it was researcher Julian Schrittwieser needling Huang about open-sourcing CUDA. There is no corporate statement. A company that spent the week being named as the beneficiary of a ban, and that doubled its political spending to $40 million, said nothing while its two largest rivals signed. Meanwhile Polymarket gives it a 90 percent chance of holding the best model at the end of August. Being the closed holdout is cheapest when you are also winning, and that is exactly the position that invites the accusation Sacks is making.

The technical substance, and the claim that reaches into the memory trade. K3 is 2.8 trillion parameters, a mixture of experts with 896 experts and 16 active per token for roughly 50 billion active, over a million tokens of context. The architecture detail that matters is Kimi Delta Attention, a linear-attention variant running three of those layers to every full-attention layer, with claimed decode up to 6.3 times faster at a million tokens.

That is where this crosses into hardware. Standard attention grows the key-value cache with context, and linear attention makes it a fixed size, so memory per user becomes a constant. Bubble Boi drew the conclusion out loud this week: less offloading, existing racks serving far more users, and therefore no justification for a new half-million-dollar server rack. He exempted high-bandwidth memory explicitly, walked the claim back on air to about 75 percent of the model being fixed, said after more research that he does not think it is as clear-cut, and dated the impact roughly two years out. He also argues it is bullish Nvidia, because linear attention keeps the GPU busy rather than idle waiting on memory. Today's Investing memo takes that argument apart properly.

On the distillation accusation, the evidence is thinner than the rhetoric. A similarity chart matching K3 to Fable 5 and to Opus 4.7 and 4.8 circulated, with the hosts themselves warning it shows correlation only. Against it: Fable 5 had been live around three weeks, frontier APIs no longer return logits, and reasoning traces ship abridged. Stella Biderman's legal point is the one to keep, that there is nothing illegal about distilling a publicly available model because outputs are not intellectual property. The White House is making a terms-of-service argument dressed as a theft argument.

The practitioner takeaway is routing rather than switching. A Cursor SQLite rebuild using a K3 planner with a cheap worker model cut cost nine to ten times against running Fable end to end. That pattern, a strong slow model for planning and a cheap fast one for execution, is where the actual savings live, and it is the same conclusion the Opus 5 reviewers reached from the other direction when they found the model performs better at medium reasoning than at maximum.

What to watch. The weights land this morning, with twenty-plus inference providers expected within days, and Ondrej's advice was to buy a drive and download them before any ban lands. CXMT lists in Shanghai the same day, which means the two Chinese challenges to American AI, one in models and one in memory, arrive within hours of each other. Grok 4.6 ships in about two weeks and 4.7 in four. US-China AI talks are scheduled for September. Anthropic is expected to go public by year end, which makes its silence this weekend a disclosure decision as much as a communications one.

Three questions decide the next month. Whether the administration converts Kratsios's accusation into an actual Entity List designation or an executive order reaching routers, because that is the difference between rhetoric and a market event. Whether Anthropic signs, stays silent, or argues publicly for restriction, since it is now the only frontier lab without a position on the record. And whether anyone ships a genuinely token-efficient open model, which was last Sunday's watch item and remains unmet, because until one exists the price of intelligence has fallen on the rate card and not yet in the invoice.
