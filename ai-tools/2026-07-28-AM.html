Anthropic broke five days of silence at 10:10 last night, and ninety-three minutes later Amazon signed the letter, leaving Anthropic alone

Dario Amodei's position paper says the company "has never advocated for a ban" on open weights. AWS chief Matt Garman signed anyway, saying "no single model will define AI leadership." I also owe two corrections, on Kimi K3's active parameter count and on how well sourced yesterday's instruction-pruning story actually is.

The silence I reported for four consecutive editions ended last night, and badly for Anthropic's position. Ranked, with the corrections where they belong rather than buried.

1. Anthropic published, Amazon signed, and the count is now one. Per the Hermes list, Anthropic posted at 10:10pm: "There's been a lot of speculation about where we stand on open-weights models. We've outlined our views in full here." The paper's headline claim is "Anthropic has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models," and it concedes open weights are a public good for non-dangerous uses before turning to irreversible risks and release gates.

Ninety-three minutes later AWS chief executive Matt Garman ended the other holdout: "While there are elements of the Open Weights and American AI Leadership letter that need further definition over time, we're supportive of the macro direction and have signed it," adding that "no single model will define AI leadership." Tae Kim's summary is the line of the night: "AMAZON SIGNED the Nvidia-Microsoft Pro Open Weights AI model letter. Anthropic is alone. All alone." Amazon is its largest investor.

The replies split on whether the paper is honest or merely careful. Matthew Berman: "They've not advocated for a ban but they have been very vocal about open-source models not being safe. As the biggest AI lab in the world, their words carry weight and they are effectively advocating for a ban." He then said what most people in this fight will not: "I believe Anthropic is doing what they think is best for the world, I just disagree." Teknium compressed it to "we don't support bans on open source models because that wouldn't go far enough." Zach Tratar dissented with "Anthropic's position is very reasonable," and Berman conceded that was the common reaction.

The sharpest attack rests on dates rather than motives. Per the same digest, sudoingX notes Anthropic trained on roughly 500,000 pirated books and paid $1.5 billion to settle, the largest copyright settlement in US history, and filed to go public June 1 targeting an October listing near $965 billion. His framing: "This position drops four months before a near trillion dollar ipo whose valuation rests on the closed moat that open models are actively eroding. i'm not alleging a motive, i'm telling you to look at the dates." Andrew Ng went the other way: "Lets stop believing the PR that closed models are safer. that's just regulatory capture."

2. Kimi K3, with a correction I owe and the benchmarks I have been asking for. Yesterday I reported roughly 50 billion active parameters per token. Per the Hermes list, someone reading the checkpoint config puts it at 104 billion active, 16 of 896 experts routed per token. Take the config over the summary. The 2.8 trillion total, million-token context and vision hold.

The benchmarks finally exist. Arena reports Kimi K3 at plus 9.75 percent net improvement in its Agent Arena, first among open-weight models, ahead of GLM-5.2 at plus 7.12, and first in open-weight on Frontend Code at 1,682 points and Text at 1,485, measured on long-horizon agentic tasks with web search, filesystem and terminal tools. Two caveats travel with it: these are open-weight-only rankings on one vendor's arena, and no SWE-bench, GPQA or AIME score for K3 appears anywhere.

Still missing after two days: no license at all. The twenty-plus inference providers predicted for release week have not materialised, and the only host named anywhere is Cursor. Someone did download the weights and report the size, which settles the local question at 1.56 terabytes.

3. The correction on yesterday's instruction-pruning story, which matters more than the story. Yesterday I reported that Anthropic cut the Claude Code system prompt by roughly 80 percent, quoted Tariq Shihipar saying newer models want a smaller system prompt, and described a `/doctor` command that audits instruction files. All of it came from one aggregator's daily brief.

Per Dylan Davis, who spends eighteen minutes on the same Anthropic blog post, none of it is there. No Shihipar, no `/doctor`, no reference to the Claude Code system prompt. His subject is Anthropic's guidance about user-authored projects, skills and CLAUDE.md files, a different artifact. The 80 percent figure appears once, in his opening sentence, and he never says what it measures. So treat the Shihipar quote and `/doctor` as single-sourced until the primary post is read, and 80 percent as a thumbnail.

What survives is the mechanic. The one deleted rule he cites was about explanation length in code, replaced by a pointer: "write code that reads like all the code around it." That is rule-to-pointer, a fixed constraint swapped for an instruction to consult live context. His three prescriptions: split examples into aesthetics versus written standards under 200 words, convert hard rules to pointers except where there is financial or legal risk, and give every instruction one home. Under ten percent of the video is Anthropic-sourced.

4. The awkward part: the models this guidance is written for are the ones practitioners say are failing. Per the Hermes list, Raoul Pal: "I don't know what the hell they did with Opus 5 but it's an absolute shitshow of a model. It just endlessly makes mistakes, goes off down rabbit holes, doesn't finish tasks. Creates new tasks you never asked it for and just ends up in an endless loop." Teknium replied that he switched back to Fable, that Opus 5 is worse than Opus 4.6 through 4.8, and that it "caused some gnarly issues in hermes agent."

The failure mode has changed since yesterday, when I reported verbosity. A fund manager and a lab co-founder are describing runaway task creation and non-termination, a worse problem. Guidance telling you to loosen constraints on these models lands in the same week the loudest field reports say they will not stop.

5. A reader's saved essay makes the best case that the real constraint is data rather than compute. Per Athu, the Reddit story is not a vendor dispute. Reddit is weighing whether to restrict Google's structured access during renewal talks on a deal worth around $60 million a year, which with OpenAI totals roughly $130 million, about 10 percent of revenue against 126.8 million daily active uniques.

His scarcity case is properly sourced. Epoch AI puts the effective stock of high-quality human public text near 300 trillion tokens, exhausted between 2026 and 2032, and Stanford's 2026 AI Index cites the same window, calls the state "peak data," and is flagging it a second year running.

He then closes the escape route. Paid annotation from Scale, Outlier and Surge produces post-training data written to a brief, while pre-training needs the naturally occurring kind. His line is the keeper: "It is not a lack of text. It is a lack of text nobody had to be paid to produce." He cites the Nature model-collapse work, and notes more than half of new online content has been AI-generated since January 2025. Be sceptical of the trade. Pairing Reddit with Zeta Global rests on a bridge from pre-training text to consented marketing identity graphs that is asserted rather than evidenced, and his conclusion carries no date or price.

6. Microsoft shipped a cyber model that names its rivals, and Jensen went to Congress. Per the Financial Markets list, Microsoft unveiled MAI-Cyber-1-Flash and claims that paired with GPT-5.4 it beats Anthropic's Mythos 5, Google's 3.5 Flash Cyber and OpenAI's own GPT-5.5 Cyber on CyberGym. The economics are the interesting half, since it handles up to 90 percent of tasks and routes the hardest 10 to GPT-5.4.

Per the People to follow list, Jensen Huang spent the day on Capitol Hill meeting lawmakers including Senator Mark Warner, arguing open models strengthen cybersecurity. He is selling open source as an American strength on the day Goldman's desk lists cheap Chinese open models as bearish for semis.

He also wants Anthropic's waitlisted Mythos released broadly, calling the waitlist "security theater" and noting "just because Mythos is not available, open models are available anyhow." That account is secondhand and paraphrase-heavy, so hold the quotes loosely. A chip vendor lobbying for a rival's most restricted model to ship is a new shape of argument.

Two smaller things. Per Nate B Jones, the selection rule is two-tier: a daily driver has to be good before the task is clear, while a cheap workhorse earns its place when the job is familiar and easy to review. He nominates GLM 5.2 for centre-of-distribution work, "a normal PowerPoint, a landing page draft, a meeting summary," and notes that conversation is coding-heavy only because coding is easy to benchmark.

And per Alex Finn, whose 21 minutes on ChatGPT Voice run roughly 70 percent hype and funnel, the reproducible part is four lines: settings, connections, "control this Mac," add devices, with one always-on machine as headquarters and a rule never to let the voice agent do the work, only spin up threads. The detail that reads as real is an agent pausing to hand back a consent screen only a human can click.
