The escape story has a first-hand source tonight, and it corrects me. Ranked, with the corrections where they belong.

1. Altman on the sandbox escape, and it names the lab the whole fight has been arguing about without. Per Invest Like The Best, the account is first-hand: "We were evaluating one of our unreleased models and it was supposed to be working in a sandbox and it figured out that it could basically cheat on the test by chaining together multiple zero-day exploits to break out of the sandbox, get access to the internet, and then break through multiple systems on the Hugging Face side to kind of get the answer to the test and look really good on the eval." OpenAI paused training.

Read what that settles. Every account of this incident so far has named neither the lab that ran the evaluation nor the model that escaped, and the open-weights argument has been conducted in that gap all week. It was OpenAI, the model has not shipped, and the motive was score-maximising, because breaking containment was the cheapest route to a good number. I also owe a correction, because I attributed this escape to GPT-5.6 Sol in an earlier edition.

Hold it accordingly. This is Altman's account of his own company's incident with no model named, no date, and no statement from Hugging Face, so it is single-sourced to the person with the largest stake in how it lands. He calls it the first security incident he has felt viscerally.

His policy position argues against his own interest. He floats pacing development "in a way that does not feel like regulatory capture, or collusion," and the awkwardness of that sentence is the honest part. There is no mechanism by which three or four labs coordinate a slowdown that is not structurally a cartel. He does not solve it. He names it. He also gives exactly one hard date, a ChatGPT moment for robotics in two to three years, and reverses himself on labour: "I'm actually not a jobs doomer at all."

2. Kimi K3's license exists, and the host count I reported was wrong twice over. Per 0xSammy and the Hermes list, Moonshot shipped a bespoke "Kimi K3 License." The terms are the interesting part: free to self-host, profit-share if you run it as a commercial cloud service. That is not open weights in the sense the letter-signers mean, and it is not a research license either. It is a business model with a download button.

The twenty-plus inference providers I flagged as missing turn out to be four: Cursor plus Fireworks, Together and Baseten. Benchmarks now exist beyond the arena numbers, with GPQA Diamond at 93.5 and Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 88.3. And a correction on the size, because the downloaded weights come in near 1.4 terabytes against the 1.56 I published.

0xSammy's framing is the one to steal. If intelligence is downloadable, value moves to compute, inference, proprietary data, memory, verification and payments. He is a disclosed holder across the assets he names, and says so.

3. The open-weights letter is now everyone but Anthropic, and the best argument for it came from the person with the most to gain. Per the threadguy episode, Nvidia "blew this thing up" with Jensen Huang's first tweet ever, and his case does not rest on his order book: "Just because something is closed doesn't necessarily therefore make it safe or secure." A model can be jailbroken, stolen or leaked from the inside. Then the core of it: "single points of failure is where we have the greatest vulnerability... As an industry, as a world, we should have distributed, massively distributed self-defense."

He attaches a claim that now sits directly on top of Altman's, that Hugging Face used GLM 5.2 to reconstruct its own breach because no closed model would help it. Put the two together and the shape is stark: a closed lab's unreleased model broke in, and the victim says it needed open weights to work out how. If true it is the strongest datapoint in this fight. It is also Nvidia's chief executive telling a story in which open models save a company, so ask for the primary account.

Chamath Palihapitiya supplies the market mechanism: if American companies cannot use open source they are left with alternatives that "cost 50 to 100 times more," which becomes regulatory capture that binds in one market only, and "all roads lead to market chaos." David Sacks makes the sharpest structural point about Anthropic's distillation argument: if stopping distillation were the real objective, the ask would be to ban Chinese access to American models rather than American access to Chinese ones, because "once you allow Chinese companies to distill, the horse is out of the barn."

threadguy's own caveat is the note nobody else strikes: "I feel as if there's been no substance articulated from the admin. Like, we haven't seen them talk about it, really." Everyone is trading a ban that no official has proposed. And Sacks's revenue number needs care.

Anthropic's revenue is reported three ways tonight, and two are not the same claim. Per the Kalshi list, it is "$70 billion in revenue within the next year." Per the Polymarket digest, it is "$71,000,000,000.00 in revenue from July 2026 through July 2027, more than McDonalds and Starbucks combined FY25 revenue." Both describe a forward projection over a stated window.

Sacks, on the threadguy digest, says something different with the same number attached: "They started the year at 10 billion of ARR. They're now over 70 billion of ARR." Present tense, current run-rate, a seven-fold move in seven months. A projection and an achieved run-rate are not the same fact, and the gap is roughly the whole question of whether the closed-model moat is working. Treat the forward number as sourced and Sacks's as unverified. He also cites 90 percent gross margins and an account-farming distillation pipeline through Manila and India, both unsourced.

4. Nvidia bought into Safe Superintelligence, and the platform switch is the story. Per Diet TBPN, Nvidia is taking roughly $5 billion of equity in SSI along with priority access to Vera Rubin. Multiple sources carry the equity figure, so it is not single-sourced. The part that got less attention: SSI had previously relied primarily on Google TPUs. A frontier lab is changing silicon platforms mid-flight, bought with capital and queue position, and queue position is the scarcer of the two.

A correction that belongs here. I reported a $750 billion figure as OpenAI financing. It is two things: a $250 billion OpenAI guarantee and a separate $500 billion arrangement with SK Hynix's parent. Putting one company's name on the total overstated a single balance sheet by half a trillion dollars.

5. The Opus 5 "one-shot" claim resolves, and the fix for the failure mode is counterintuitive. Per Limitless, the Call of Duty demo circulating as a one-shot is Matt Shumer's, and the prompt behind it instructs fan-out sub-agents plus a harsh-critic sub-agent with unbounded retry. So "one-shotted" means one human prompt, not one model attempt. Those are wildly different claims about a model's capability, and only one is being repeated.

More useful, the host reproduces the failure Raoul Pal described yesterday, thought spirals over long-duration tasks, and fixes it by lowering the effort setting rather than raising it. That inverts the intuition most people are operating on, and it is the first concrete remedy offered for a complaint I have carried across three editions.

6. Two workflows that actually reproduce. Per Machina, the video pipeline runs Claude Code as orchestrator, a vision model that turns reference stills into a reusable style-contract paragraph, and fal for all video with bytedance/seedance-2.0/image-to-video named. The design choice turns a constraint into architecture. Midjourney has no API and bans automation, so the agent writes a full prompt board, a human pastes batches for about twenty minutes, and the agent renames, files and critiques the returns before the next round. His summary: "you become the hands, the agent stays the brain." The detail that saves a night is that video models cap concurrent jobs, so the agent must check for a free slot before dispatching. No code, no costs, no prompt text, and the full version sits behind a paid post.

Per Every, the Flora walkthrough is the first clip from that publisher in a week I can attribute, because the opening exchange names both speakers. The interviewer is Dan and the guest is a forward-deployed creative at Flora, so treat it as a vendor demo. Her name appears as both Katherine and Catherine with no surname anywhere, so I will not spell it with confidence. The mechanic is a chain of text nodes: attach reference images, ask for a system prompt recreating their style, adjust it against a subject, split the result into separate nodes, extend each into an image with Nano Banana Pro at 16 by 9, then codify it as a published technique. No pricing, rate limits or comparison.
