Two days ago four labs refused to sign the open-weights letter, and tonight OpenAI and Google have signed it, which leaves Anthropic standing alone with the best model and no allies

Chamath put the only tradeable claim on the table, arguing a US ban on open source would crater Anthropic's and OpenAI's own valuations rather than protect them. Opus 5's backlash hardened from grumbling into allocation, one reviewer now gives it zero percent of his work, and a second independent source says the fix is to turn its reasoning down.

Friday the story was that Nvidia wrote a letter and the four frontier labs all declined it. That lasted about 24 hours. Ranked.

1. OpenAI signed, then Google signed, and now there is exactly one holdout. Per tae kim, "OpenAI tells me they signed the Nvidia-Microsoft open-source/open-weights AI model letter today. What an amazing turn of events. Your move, Anthropic!" Sundar Pichai then signed for Google citing Gemma, along with Michael Dell and Arcee, and Sam Altman said he wants the US to win "in AI both in open source and proprietary models."

Matthew Berman reduced it to four words: "Only one company left…" Per threadguy's guest, the sharpest counter is aimed at the labs' identity: "if you're an AI lab, you're a lab. Your job is to innovate. If you can't innovate, you're not going to be a lab for long."

The only Anthropic voice tonight is a researcher needling Jensen Huang. Per the Hermes list, Julian Schrittwieser posted "I'm so excited that @JensenHuang is a believer in open source now, looking forward to the CUDA and GPU driver open source release." There is no corporate statement from Anthropic anywhere.

Follow the incentives rather than the principles. Per Packy McCormick, this is "$8 trillion worth of market cap coming out in support of open-weight models," and the frame is Joel Spolsky's rule that smart companies commoditize their complements. Nvidia sells chips, Microsoft sells products, Palantir wants to disintermediate models in the enterprise, CrowdStrike needs defenders at parity with attackers, Google gets cheaper ad serving. The investable claim is that Anthropic and OpenAI cannot run away with the frontier as a duopoly.

2. Chamath's version is the one with a number in it, and it points the opposite way from what you would expect. Per Chamath Palihapitiya, a ban on open source is unambiguously market-negative: "If the United States government intervenes, it will tank the stock market. Not debatable." The mechanism is cost of goods sold. Restrict an enterprise buyer like Coca-Cola to two approved vendors and its AI input costs run 50 to 100 times the next-best alternative, so Coke gets rerated while non-US competitors keep the cheap input.

Then he turns it on the labs. "This is where Anthropic and OpenAI need to understand. If the government comes in and actually tells you that there's no open source, their valuation will crater." Ban-derived revenue is propped up rather than demand-driven, and it is regulatory capture that works in one market only.

David Sacks supplies the legal edge and names the defendant. Per Sacks, he refuses the China-defense framing ("I'm not defending Chinese companies. I'm defending American developers") and his example is concrete: Cursor's Composer 2 was post-trained on Kimi 2.5 using Cursor's own coding data. Once weights are public "it's not a Chinese model anymore. No data is going back to China." Banning American companies from forking them would "put a dagger through the heart of the entire American open source ecosystem. And that is exactly what Anthropic wants because they do not want to have the competition."

The other side says none of this touches capex. Per threadguy's guest, frontier labs are priced on frontier capability rather than parity, so if the next Gemini discovers a drug "what's the value of that Gemini model? It's whatever the value of the drugs are." His falsification is Chinese models producing capabilities America cannot match, years out.

3. The Opus 5 backlash hardened from grumbling into allocation, and it is zero. This morning the split resolved along task class. Tonight it resolved into a number. Per Every, the allocation is 80 percent GPT-5.6, 20 percent Fable and 0 percent Opus 5. Two slots exist, daily driver and heavy artillery, and Opus 5 wins neither.

The complaint is about behaviour rather than capability.

  • "A poor man's Fable. It has all of Fable's personality, but really not its top end," per Every.
  • Pushy and argumentative. "You can get away with that if you're super smart. If you're not, then it's just more annoying."
  • Migration is not free. Opus 4.8 users will find it harder and "a lot of your skills you're going to have to think about rewriting."
  • Do not switch. "If you're a Codex user, I would not switch. 5.6 in Codex is still the gold standard for knowledge work."

Per Every, the diagnosis is strategic. Anthropic builds one enormous model to train its others and chases recursive self-improvement, while OpenAI pivoted from size to post-training after GPT-4.5 flopped. The barb is the line of the night: your model ends up "not really a super genius but they have all the attitude of a super genius." Codex is adding roughly a million users a day.

One caveat, which they raise themselves: GPT-5, 5.1 and 5.2 were all hard to like on arrival and turned out powerful, so the real verdict lands in a couple of weeks. This is a post-training usability problem, the fixable kind.

And the fix arrives again from someone who got there separately. Per Every, Kieran prefers Opus 5 at medium or low reasoning, while the host admits "almost all my testing was on high or max, cuz I'm like oh these are hard tasks." The rule generalizes: "the thing to start dialing up and down is the thinking level," not the model family. Blunter: "Opus is a smart model that does better when it thinks less." Two independent sources now, same counterintuitive instruction, whole current Claude generation. That is the most actionable thing today.

The market disagrees with the reviewers too. Per ArkkDaily, Polymarket gives Anthropic a 90 percent chance of the best model at the end of August, though Novogratz's caution is fair: most of America is not using AI yet.

Commercially, per Kris Torrington, Fable 5 became permanent on July 20, and permanent is not unlimited. Per his companion clip, Max and Team Premium get it, Pro does not, and it can eat at most 50 percent of the weekly allowance.

5. Claude Cowork is the sleeper, and its four-part prompt is the steal. Per Dylan Davis, whose receipt is Claude renaming 400 files while he made coffee, the idea is "point, don't paste." Browser chat caps you near five to eight attachments. One folder per task, and skip projects on desktop.

His prompt structure has four parts and generalizes to any long agent run:

  • Destination, the outcome rather than the steps.
  • A binary finish line: "done when every file has a home and a clear name."
  • A guardrail that flips the incentive, his favourite being "it's okay to say you don't know" plus a literal `not sure` folder.
  • A receipt, files found against files touched. Found 662, touched 605 tells you 57 were skipped without auditing by hand.

The rest copies wholesale. Escalate low to medium to high in a fresh chat and never above high, because extra and max just overthink and burn money. Go unattended only after a task survives 5 to 20 manual runs. Build skills by doing the work manually, then saying "turn this process into a skill" in that conversation. Add a `lessons.md` Claude reads first, and cap instruction files under 100 lines.

6. Four notes from the week's practitioners.

  • Nobody was measuring AI spend, and Fable's launch exposed it. Per Granola's founder, somebody said "I think I spent a couple thousand today," prompting "do we even know how much we're spending?" The eye-watering number is not theirs: Kieran, who runs Kora, is on pace for about $1 million a year in Fable credits. The response was measurement rather than a cap. The failure mode was never overspending, it was not knowing.
  • Stop rebuilding internal tools. Per Every, the build-your-own phase ended in "this sucks." Trust PostHog, Notion and Slack through the model over MCP and never open the app.
  • Invert your knowledge system. Per Dan Koe, retention is downstream of a project, so capture nothing without an output that needs it. His build is Obsidian plus Claude with two skills, save-an-idea and process-my-inbox.
  • The video stack that works today, per AI Central: Suno 5.5 for stems, Kling 3.0 Omni over Seedance 2.0 (which reverts to trained-in faces whatever reference you supply), Clarify for lipsync.

The one to watch is agents operating your accounts rather than your documents. Per Granola's chief executive, he told Codex to fix his internet plan after moving floors. It opened Verizon in its in-app browser, logged in, opened a live chat with an agent "probably also using AI," priced the plan and was primed to refuse hidden fees.
