Anthropic answered the letter it would not sign, and the choke point is hosting

Dario Amodei published three clarifications and three proposals instead of signing, Amazon and Apple are the other two holdouts, and a separate petition called Pacing the Frontier now carries 1,224 names including his.

ModelsAgentsToolingLocal/Open

Last night the open-weights letter was everyone but Anthropic, and Sam Altman was floating a way to pace development that would not feel like regulatory capture. Tonight Anthropic answered with a document of its own, a second letter surfaced with 1,224 signatures and both big labs behind it, and somebody said the word capture out loud. Ranked, starting with what changes for September.

1. Anthropic replaced a signature with a position, and the enforcement question finally has a mechanism attached. Per Diet TBPN, Dario Amodei published a direct response to the Nvidia-organised open letter. Three clarifications: Anthropic has never advocated a blanket ban on open-weight models, the United States must beat authoritarian governments to powerful AI, and sufficiently capable models can be turned to cyber or biological attack. Three proposals: keep the chip sanctions on China, crack down on industrial-scale distillation, and put every sufficiently capable model, open or closed, through mandatory safety testing. Demis Hassabis has argued that third point publicly, so Google DeepMind sits on that side of it too.

The phrase doing the work is "policy intervention," and Amodei never defines it. The hosts reason out what it could be. A tax, a tariff, a fine, or the one that follows logically, pressure on anyone hosting or serving weights that can be shown to be distilled.

Then the part to keep. The reachable point is not the download, it is the building. Torrented weights cannot be recalled, but a model needs racks and power to serve anyone at scale, and any American company with a hall full of NVL72s has business registrations and an existing list of things it already declines to host.

That sharpens a call I have been carrying since Monday. I said September is when Washington decides and that a federal procurement ban was the only instrument with real teeth. Procurement is not the instrument. Hosting is. A procurement ban reaches what the government buys, and serving restrictions reach what everybody else is allowed to run.

Amodei pre-empts the obvious objection inside the letter, conceding his policy would shelter American AI companies from competition and saying that is not his aim. Three major companies still have not signed: Amazon, Apple and Anthropic. The first two either sell other people's models for a living or build physical products, so neither needs this fight.

2. The pacing petition carries 1,224 names and both labs, and the capture accusation arrived the same day. Per the Hermes list, "Pacing the Frontier" is signed by more than 1,200 frontier-lab employees, among them Amodei, Anthropic co-founder Jared Kaplan, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki and Meta chief scientist Shengjia Zhao. The ask is that Washington build international tooling to deliberately slow automated AI research if capability gains outrun oversight. Anthropic endorsed it and tied it to its own recursive self-improvement work. OpenAI's account posted the same position inside ninety minutes.

David Ondrej calls it regulatory capture. Per his show, the case is that both labs ship closed models, both hold the lead, and the America-versus-China framing is cover for closed versus open. He offers no documentary evidence of intent, so hold it as an accusation and not a finding.

The sturdier objection is the one his co-host makes in passing. A pacing agreement binds one jurisdiction. China ships open weights monthly and will not honour it. On that reading the instrument does not slow the frontier, it relocates part of it, and the labs that asked for it keep their lead by default.

Nous Research is the real fault line, offering conditional support rather than opposition. Per Shannon Sands, pacing is fine provided it is not a pause, he wants effort pushed toward user intent, interpretability and prose quality over "2% more on SWE-bench," and he withdraws entirely "at a whiff of 'and therefore we're banning open models and research in general'."

My lean: 65/35 that nothing with teeth ships by the end of September. The 35 is the hosting route, because it is the one that needs no new statute.

3. Claude Mythos did real cryptanalysis, and the bill came to about $100,000 a run. Per the same list, Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, working in an agentic harness with code execution, found a nontrivial automorphism in the lattice underneath HAWK, a NIST post-quantum signature candidate. It drops HAWK-256 key recovery from roughly 2^64 to 2^38. Separately the model built a fingerprinting technique it called a Möbius Bridge that speeds a meet-in-the-middle attack on a seven-round AES-128 variant by 200 to 800 times.

The process figures matter as much as the results. HAWK took about 60 hours with intermittent human guidance, the AES work ran largely unattended across several days, and each cost roughly $100,000 in API compute. Anthropic shipped the papers, the chain-of-thought traces, demonstration code, and a new benchmark called CryptanalysisBench. Nothing deployed is affected.

Hold that against section one, because it is the same week. The lab arguing that sufficiently capable models enable cyber attack published its own model finding a genuine weakness in a post-quantum candidate for the cost of a graduate student. It is the best evidence anyone has produced for the pacing case, and it is self-produced, a fact that cuts both ways.

Read it for exactly what it measures, one open model in one harness comparison, and note that the fastest scaffold and the most frugal one were different products. Nine times the cost for fewer solves is still not a rounding error, and it is the strongest case yet that scaffolding now moves your bill more than model choice does.

5. Two camps on token spend, and they are dividing by different denominators. Nate B Jones instrumented himself and produced the number of the night. In one working day his tracker logged 3.77 billion tokens through his Codex workspace across 143 threads, and 3.59 billion of them, about 96 percent, were reused input. Per his walkthrough, every turn resends the conversation from the top, so one failed answer makes the retry carry all of it a second time. He adds Anthropic's own published figure that a setup with GitHub, Slack, Sentry and Grafana connected spends roughly 55,000 tokens on tool definitions before the model does anything at all.

His structural point is the one that survives contact. A skill cannot shrink the call it sits inside, because by the time the model reads the skill the standing instructions and tool definitions are already in the envelope. Hence Ringer, a local intermediary that acts on a request before it leaves the machine.

Every's compound engineering team takes the opposite posture. Per their clip, the phrase is "token efficient but not token deficient," and the metric is cost to ship, so when $7 finishes the feature and $5 leaves you cleaning up behind it, spend the $7.

These two are not fighting. Jones minimises waste per call, Every maximises outcome per dollar, and reused input is waste under both. Kill the 96 percent, then let it rain tokens on the part that ships something.

6. Three things to install this week, named.

  • `/doctor` in Claude Code reads 30-plus days of your logs to find skills you have never once invoked and removes them, de-bloats CLAUDE.md, and pre-approves commands you keep granting by hand. Per Chase AI, the log-driven audit is the novel piece, because it prunes on usage evidence rather than a guess.
  • last-30-days, an open-source Claude Code skill at 55,000 GitHub stars, sits between a shallow web search and a multi-million-token deep research run. Per the deep dive, it pulls full transcripts and comments from Reddit, Hacker News, X, YouTube, TikTok, GitHub and Polymarket, ranks by cross-platform repetition rather than upvotes, and runs about ten cents a go.
  • `/cpov oracle this` from Every's compound engineering repo has Claude write a neutral restatement of the decision with no opinion attached, then ships that to Grok and Codex. Per the clip, pasting your own model's answer into a rival primes the rival, so the unopinionated restatement is the entire trick.

One more for the local pile. Per the Hermes list, Unsloth compressed Kimi K3 from 1.56 terabytes to 594 gigabytes, a 62 percent cut, with the one-bit quantisation holding about 78.9 percent accuracy. It runs on a Mac Studio with 128GB of memory. The strongest open model on the board became desk-side hardware in a day. That is what the whole policy fight above is actually about.
