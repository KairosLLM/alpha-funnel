Both neoclouds beat, Burry got squeezed, and my memory reversal was wrong

Nebius put up 454 percent revenue growth and CoreWeave broke a five-quarter losing streak on earnings day, while Goldman modelled DRAM prices up 250 to 300 percent next year.

Ai-InfraSemisFinancialsEnergy

Two days ago I reversed the memory call to compression on the argument that five-year contracts had capped the pricing lever. Today Goldman published a DRAM price forecast that makes that look like the wrong read of the wrong market.

Market context. Per the Kalshi morning list, Tuesday closed with the S&P down 0.3 percent, the Nasdaq 100 down 0.6 and the Russell 2000 up 0.3. Today opened green on the in-line CPI Macro has, then chopped on the lightest volume in thirteen sessions, with Fear and Greed at 62. The drawdowns underneath the index are the part to hold onto: per the Financial Markets list, from all-time highs $META is off 27 percent, $GOOGL 16.4, $AAPL 13 and $MSFT 10.8, with $NVDA down only 5.5.

  • Miss. My Nebius call carried Rick Orford's bearish framing at 105.5 times sales with Burry short at $211.77. Nebius beat and ran more than 20 percent. Section one.

1. Both neoclouds beat, and the pricing disclosure is the real news. Per the Stocks morning list, CoreWeave did $2.6 billion of revenue against $2.56 billion expected, adjusted EBITDA of $1.5 billion against $1.43 billion at a 59 percent margin, with backlog at about $104 billion, up 46 percent, and full-year guidance raised to $12.4 to $13.2 billion. Per the People to follow morning list, earnings came in at minus $1.14 against minus $1.45 expected, a 21 percent beat, on a net loss that widened to $626 million.

Per the Market morning list, it ran from minus 3 percent to plus 14.39 percent within an hour, its first green earnings reaction in six tries after five straight of minus 11.4, minus 18.5, minus 16.3, minus 20.8 and minus 2.5.

Nebius was the bigger number. Per the People to follow list:

The disclosure that matters is what a megawatt costs. Per the Stocks list, mid-term deals run $20 to $25 million per megawatt with half to sixty percent of the capex paid upfront, short deals run $40 to $50 million, and the first capacity auction cleared 15 percent above the highest price it had ever charged for Blackwell. Management said it could sell all of 2027 today and is choosing not to.

That is the answer to the depreciation argument, and it does not require anyone to win a debate about useful life. Burry's short says these companies overstate profits by depreciating GPUs too slowly. Nebius is being paid more per megawatt each quarter and collecting most of the capex before the machines run. Per Edelbridge Alpha, its $775 million facility at SOFR plus 250 covers more than 100 percent of the capex it funds and is repeatable against more than $40 billion of contracted revenue. A business that pre-sells capacity above cost does not need the accounting to be generous.

The counterweight is what Nvidia's $500 billion actually is. Per TBPN, the mechanism is securitization: Nvidia supplies depreciation insurance up to 25 percent plus reference designs that make data centres fungible, so the debt can be tranched into investment-grade paper and sold to pension funds and insurers. The banks are swapping project-specific credit risk for sector-wide risk and collecting fees for the trip. Per Milk Road AI, the money is "not in 2026 or 2027, but over time," and across six sources today nobody produced a per-firm number or a first-dollar date. Treat it as a distribution channel that has been built, not as demand that has been committed.

2. I generalised a NAND concession to DRAM, and that was the error. On Monday I reversed this call because Chip Stock Investor, holding SanDisk through a drawdown, conceded that five-year hyperscaler agreements had ended the wild average selling prices. That concession was real and it was about NAND.

Per the Stocks morning list, the DRAM market is going the other way and the sell side is not being subtle about it. Goldman revised DRAM price forecasts to up 250 to 300 percent in 2026 and up 300 to 350 percent in 2027. Bernstein models average selling prices reaching $2.23 a gigabyte by the end of 2027, roughly five times the 2025 level. Micron posted an 81.2 percent operating margin.

Per the Stocks list, the mechanism is capacity rather than sentiment. A gigabyte of high-bandwidth memory takes three times the wafer capacity of standard DRAM, so every line converted to HBM removes conventional supply at the same time and you get a shortage in both at once. Operating margins across all three makers run 70 to 80 percent. The confirming detail is small and hard to argue with: Google raised the Pixel 11 by $100 to $899 and cited a severe memory crunch.

So the call goes back to 60/40 that multiples expand first, and I am flagging it as low conviction rather than pretending this is insight. I have moved this three times in seven days. A call that moves three times in a week is one I do not have an edge on, and the honest label is a view rather than a position. What I got wrong was treating one company's NAND contract structure as the whole memory complex.

3. Goldman bought an ETF issuer, and it is the second one this year. Per Eric Balchunas, Goldman Sachs is acquiring Neos ETFs for $2.3 billion. Neos is only a few years old and got big fast, at $32 billion, mostly in covered-call monthly income funds. Goldman bought Innovator, the buffered-ETF house, immediately before it, and Balchunas reads the pair as a bet on what he calls boomer candy.

Two acquisitions in a row in defined-outcome and income products is a house buying what sells when investors stop trusting the index. That is a read on flows rather than markets, from a firm that spent this morning declaring a September hold.

4. Texas just cut its own power forecast in half, the first hard brake on the buildout. Per the Polymarket morning list, Texas cut its 2027 power demand growth forecast to 6 percent from 14 after auditing data centre interconnection requests. Per the Stocks list, ERCOT has paused new power deals to Texas data centres outright.

Set that against what power is fetching. Galaxy leased 800 megawatts to CoreWeave at $1.2 billion a year, and its Helios II block of 830 megawatts is expected to clear above $1.4 billion.

The audit is the interesting half. If the queues were inflated by speculative requests, the demand figure everyone has been underwriting was inflated too, and the constraint moves from generation to permission. Names holding energised capacity get more valuable and names holding a pipeline get less. That is a sorting mechanism rather than a sector call.
