SpaceX printed a beat, tripled its float, and rose fifteen percent anyway

A 34 percent short interest that never squeezed on the print, a funding gap near $350 billion, and roughly a thousand special purpose vehicles nobody has mapped.

Ai-InfraIndustrials

This channel covered SpaceX in every edition it has published since the split. That is not because the ticker is always doing something. It is because one company ran a complete cycle in five sessions: the most crowded short in the market, a beat nobody had modelled, the largest lockup expiry of the year, and a fifteen percent rally on the day the supply arrived.

The interesting part is that the consensus was wrong at every single stage, including mine.

Four editions, four different reasons to be wrong.

1. The print said the business is real and the balance sheet is not finished

Per the All-In Podcast, revenue was $7.8 billion, up 92 percent year over year and 67 percent sequentially, against $18 billion for all of last year. EPS came in at $0.09 against an expected 26-cent loss, with a $500 million net loss against an expected $2 billion.

Starlink is the part that already works: 12 million subscribers, doubled year over year, at $66 a month and growing 20 percent sequentially, approaching a 24 million run rate. David Friedberg's extrapolation is $40 billion of revenue and $30 billion of free cash flow inside a year, which at 30 times would make Starlink alone a trillion-dollar company within 18 months.

The guidance is where it stops being a satellite business. Per Limitless, Elon Musk put $100 billion of annual recurring revenue by year end "if we do nothing" and pulled the $1 trillion revenue year forward from 2031 to 2030. Morgan Stanley's 2030 estimate is $325 billion. Those two numbers are not in the same universe, and the gap between them is the entire investment debate.

2. The unlock was the setup, and everyone including me had the direction backwards

Going in, the mechanics looked one-sided. S3 had 95 percent of borrowable stock out on loan and short interest at 34 percent of float. I read that as a squeeze and went 55/45 that the stock would trade higher on Thursday. It closed at its lowest price in company history instead, having shed roughly $1.6 trillion of market value since the June high.

Then Friday happened. Per the Stocks list, it ran from about $108 to $132 on the day after 911.5 million shares came free, traded 1.7 million option contracts on unlock day itself (second only to Nvidia across the whole market), and finished back above Meta's capitalisation. LuxAlgo's summary was the correct one: new rule, SpaceX unlocks are bullish.

The lesson is not that I was directionally wrong. It is that I put a date on a mechanical call. Supply cliffs are knowable in size and unknowable in timing, and a 24-hour window on a structural event is a coin flip dressed as analysis.

The supply is not finished. Per the People to follow list, the float went from 5 percent to 20 percent on Thursday and reaches roughly 40 percent by December 9, with tranches near 7 percent on August 21 and again on September 10, then smaller blocks through the autumn. Musk himself becomes eligible around 46 percent in June 2027. Tom Lee's framing is the cleanest: the company raised $18 billion in its life, is worth north of $1.4 trillion, floated $75 billion at the IPO, and over $1 trillion of stock unlocks by year end.

3. The bull case

The compute economics are genuinely better than the comparables, and that is measurable rather than rhetorical. SpaceX generates $30 million to $50 million of annualised revenue per active megawatt. CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN sit at $9.4 million to $10.4 million. Digital Realty and Equinix sit at $3.5 million to $4.4 million. SemiAnalysis puts SpaceX at roughly nine times revenue for a gigawatt of GB300 against the neocloud five-year infrastructure average.

The payback compressed. The CFO says newly deployed compute pays back inside a single calendar year, against three to four years for a typical neocloud and fifteen to twenty for real estate. A few months ago the company itself was modelling four.

Capacity is contracted and scaling. 1.4 gigawatts today, about 2 by year end, 5 to 10 next year, with line of sight to 20. At $30 million to $50 million per megawatt per year, a 6 gigawatt incremental add is worth roughly $300 billion of annual revenue on Brad Gerstner's arithmetic. SemiAnalysis models a path to $305 billion of revenue by 2027 with $235 billion, or 77 percent, from AI compute.

The sponsorship is real. JPMorgan raised its target to $240, Morgan Stanley carries $300, Argus upgraded to Buy after the quarter, Cathie Wood holds 4,051,080 shares, and Alphabet's own equity book is 95.1 percent SpaceX.

4. The bear case, and it is arithmetic rather than sentiment

The conclusion that follows is uncomfortable and it is the same structure as the OpenAI campus: Nvidia customer financing has to come into play. The chip supplier underwrites the buyer, and SpaceX has just committed to Nvidia exclusively on Vera Rubin. Gavin Baker's version is worse. At 8 gigawatts and roughly $100 billion per gigawatt, capex is $800 billion from this company alone, and component costs have already doubled in four months.

Capex is running 39 percent above expectations with no 2027 guidance. A company spending $2.35 per dollar of revenue and declining to guide the following year is asking for trust on the single largest line in the model.

The supply calendar is public and unfinished. Roughly 20 percent more of the company becomes tradable between August 21 and December 9. The Meta 2012 analogue is the honest comparable: down about 50 percent at the 91-day lockup and still down 36 percent a year later.

And there is a layer underneath that nobody has mapped. Per Diet TBPN, bankers estimate at least 1,000 special purpose vehicles hold SpaceX stock. One has already failed publicly. Late Stage Management told an investor it had sold his exposure in 2024 at roughly $105 a share, before a five-for-one split, while his portal and his 2025 tax document still showed 2,500 shares. He put in $17,250, believes the stake was worth over $300,000 at the June IPO, has filed an SEC complaint, and thinks more than 100 others are affected. Three sales executives connected to the firm pleaded guilty in February and March inside a $528 million scheme that hid markups of 10 to 100 percent and diverted roughly $88 million.

That is not a valuation risk. It is a supply risk of unknown size sitting in vehicles with no disclosure obligation, arriving into a stock whose float is already tripling.

What to do with it

Own it as a compute business, not a launch business. Connectivity and AI produce all the money. Space is break even and it is the part everyone talks about.

Let the calendar work. The tranche dates are published. There is no informational edge in front-running a supply schedule everyone can read, and Friday demonstrated that positioning into these events is more dangerous than the events themselves. The next two are August 21 and September 10.

Size for a 4-beta outcome. This is a $1.4 trillion company that has moved 30 percent in a week in both directions this month.

The single number to watch is the 2027 capex guide, whenever it arrives. That converts the $350 billion gap from an analyst estimate into a disclosed obligation, and it is the moment the vendor-financing question gets answered in public.

Watchlist for the week ahead. $SPCX into the August 21 tranche. $NBIS reports Wednesday at 105 times sales into Michael Burry's fresh short, and it is the cleanest read on whether the market still pays for neocloud growth. $MU and $SNDK, where the contracted floor held all week and the tape ignored it. $RKLB reports next week after the $8 billion Iridium deal flipped it to net debt. And $GRAB Monday and $SMCI Tuesday, both of which come due on this channel's book.
