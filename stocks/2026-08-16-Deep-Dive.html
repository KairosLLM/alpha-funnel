Memory stopped being a hope and became a number, with a new leg

SanDisk guided to 80 percent gross margins on $94 billion of contracts, Goldman modelled DRAM up 300 percent, and Washington handed Micron a tailwind.

SemisAi-InfraFinancials

This channel wrote about the memory trade in every edition it published this week, and for six days the argument was whether a five-year contract structure capped the pricing lever. By Friday that argument had an answer, and it came from Washington, SanDisk and the sell side at once. This memo is the whole case, both sides, and what I would actually do with the names.

The through-line is that the bear case was real and specific, and the bull case answered it with a different market than the bear was arguing about.

1. The concession was about NAND, and the shortage is in DRAM

Sunday's bear case was the strongest single thing this week said against the trade, and it came from a holder. Chip Stock Investor, down about 40 percent on SanDisk, said the really wild average selling prices on SSDs are over now that SanDisk and the other memory companies have signed five-year agreements with the hyperscalers. Take-or-pay at a fixed band protects the downside and removes the upside, and multiple expansion needs the upside.

That concession was real and it was about NAND. The whole reason I spent two days moving my own call to compression was that I generalised one company's NAND contract structure onto DRAM, a different market with different contracts. Goldman then published DRAM price forecasts up 250 to 300 percent in 2026 and 300 to 350 percent in 2027, and Bernstein models average selling prices at $2.23 a gigabyte by the end of 2027, roughly five times the 2025 level.

The mechanism is capacity. A gigabyte of high-bandwidth memory takes three times the wafer capacity of standard DRAM, so every line converted to HBM removes conventional supply at the same time, and you get a shortage in both at once. Operating margins across all three makers run 70 to 80 percent, and the confirming detail is small and hard to argue with: Google raised the Pixel 11 by $100 to $899 and cited a severe memory crunch.

2. SanDisk put a number on it, and the number is 80 percent through 2030

Thursday's investor day was not a story, it was a guide. SanDisk is targeting roughly 80 percent non-GAAP gross margins through fiscal 2030 on about $94 billion of locked long-term contracts, with multi-year agreements already covering half of fiscal 2027 bit supply and two-thirds by 2028. JPMorgan resumed coverage at overweight with a $2,250 target and the view that the contracts hold margins even at floor pricing. The stock is up about 50 percent since the fund that blew up on it liquidated.

Wednesday's architecture is the part that matters for the debate. HBF, SanDisk's NAND repurposed as AI memory, matches high-bandwidth memory at 12.8 terabytes per second of bandwidth while using one-eighth the GPU resources at one-quarter the footprint, at one-eighth the capex. Sell-side targets are running back to $3,000 from the current $1,404.

The skeptical read did not go away, and it came from the desks. Some call the HBF marketing and divide the addressable market by two to four. That debate is live, not settled, and it is the whole reason this is a 60/40 rather than a conviction.

3. Micron is the trade's front foot, and it finally has a catalyst

Micron closed Friday back above its 50-day line for the first time in about three weeks, with the resistance ladder at 1022, 1064, 1082, 1121 and 1155 and 991 the overnight gap target. It hit $987 premarket and cleared the $965 target the house had been tracking. The structural numbers underneath are the ones that matter: about 70 percent of hyperscaler capex is going to memory, projected 2027 spend is $761 billion, and demand is growing roughly 200 percent a year against 20 percent supply, with UBS modelling DRAM undersupplied until the second quarter of 2028.

Friday added the tailwind nobody had priced. The White House is advising Apple to stop buying memory chips from China, a direct reroute of demand to the US and Korean makers. And BofA put a number on the bull case: 80 percent gross margins through 2030 and $236 of EPS in fiscal 2030, a 34 percent CAGR against a consensus that has earnings peaking next year and shrinking after. At 15 to 20 times, that lands between $3,500 and $4,700 against a stock under $1,000.

4. The bull case

The shortage is structural and dated. HBM conversion shorting conventional supply, DRAM undersupplied to Q2 2028, and prices up 40 to 50 percent with a 200 percent demand growth rate against 20 percent supply. This is a supply problem that takes years to fix, not a quarter.

The contracts are a floor, not just a cap. SanDisk has $94 billion locked through 2030 at roughly 80 percent margins. The take-or-pay structure that caps the wild upside is the same structure that guarantees the downside. Nobody pays more for earnings pre-sold at a floor, and the floor is 80 percent gross margins. That is a good place to be wrong from.

The tailwinds are stacking. Apple off Chinese memory, Nvidia earnings on August 26 as the next catalyst, and the December 6 buyback-and-dividend lift that has been this name's first hard dated catalyst in months. The setup into Nvidia is about as clean as the AI trade gets.

5. The bear case

The marketing-versus-real debate is genuine. If HBF is real, SanDisk trades at a fraction of its worth. If it is largely a story, the $3,000 targets are a rerating nobody asked for. The desks that called the divide by two to four were not dismissed this week, they were the ones holding the argument.

The concession was honest and it still stands for NAND. Rick Orford's Micron bull case on Monday retreated from pricing to automotive and robotics, and he conceded analysts expect margins to normalize once supply catches up with demand. A bull case that changes the subject is a concession. Micron trades at 9.2 times earnings against SK Hynix at 19, and cheap is not the same as early.

The froth is the counterweight to every bull case. Margin debt at a record near $1.5 trillion, the Shiller CAPE near 41, and a memory complex that has already run. A stock that has run has farther to fall if the cycle breaks, and the leverage is where the fragility lives.

The arbitration

Both cases are correct about different markets. The bear is right about NAND and about the multiple. The bull is right that the DRAM shortage is structural and that the contracts underwrite it. I moved this call three times in seven days, the honest sign that it is a view rather than a position, and it sits at 60/40 that multiples expand before they compress.

What would change my mind: a SanDisk quarter that misses the 80 percent margin guide, or a DRAM price print that breaks the Goldman trajectory. What keeps me at 60/40: the tape has not turned, spot is still ripping, and the next hard catalyst is eight sessions out.

What to do with it

Own the structural DRAM names through the cycle and let the levels run. Micron into the 991 confirmation toward 1022, with the buyback lift in December as the dated catalyst. SanDisk on the $94 billion contract base, sized for the marketing debate.

Respect the levels and the calendar. The resistance ladder is published and Nvidia earnings is the next referee. A stock at $1,000 on a $965-cleared target is a stock that needs the next leg to confirm, not one to chase.

Watchlist for the week ahead. $MU into Nvidia earnings and the December lift. $SNDK on the contract base and the HBF debate. $NBIS, where the neocloud earnings this week validated the same demand picture from the compute side. $CRWV on the margin question now answered and the funding question not. And the optics complex, $AAOI and $LITE and $COHR, which are the physical layer that has not yet had the squeeze the memory names already have.
