The long end is still blowing out and retail just made it worse

The 30-year auctioned at the highest yield since 2001 into a 0.6 percent retail drop, gold keeps taking the hedge trade, and Japan is the next shoe.

RatesInflationDollarCommoditiesGeopolitics

The long end was the story Friday and it is the story again Monday, only now it is colliding with the weakest retail print in over a year. That collision is the day's driver, and the dollar and gold are already voting on the side of it. This is the session that turned the regime tension into a direction.

Market context. The 30-year auctioned Monday at 5.216 percent, the highest yield on a sale since 2001, against a spot that sits near 5.3 percent. The 10-year is at 4.69 percent. Retail sales fell 0.6 percent in July, the worst month since May 2025, yet the long end did not bid. That is the whole message. Gold is at $4,410 and re-accelerating, the dollar is rolling over from 101, and Japan's 10-year hit 2.93 percent, the highest since 1996, with an 80 percent chance of a September hike priced. The Fed minutes land Wednesday.

The scoreboard. The week's due catalyst on this book is the Fed minutes Wednesday, the call that decides whether the 30-year at 5.3 percent is regime change or a pullback. The session moved the setup: July retail at minus 0.6 percent cut September hike odds toward 30 percent, so the long end, not September, is the event the minutes are really testing.

1. The long end is the regime and retail could not save it. The 30-year auctioned at 5.216 percent, the highest since 2001, and the longer-duration bond ETF is down 63 percent from its March 2020 peak as the 30-year went from an all-time low of 0.8 percent then to 5.3 percent now.

The signal in Monday's tape is what did not happen: retail sales fell 0.6 percent in July, the sharpest drop since May 2025, and the long end still would not rally on it. A bond market that cannot catch a bid on soft data is a market telling you nominal growth is running hotter than the prints, and that is the funding cost story underneath everything. The US debt sits closing in on $40 trillion and corporate equity is more than 400 percent of GDP, nearly double the dot-com peak. Capital Flows has the framing the week needed: September is a distraction, the long end is the event.

2. Gold is the hedge that moved, and BofA is leaning on it. The split the house flagged all week is now two-sided. BofA's Michael Hartnett calls the trade "long gold," as the best hedge against dollar weakness, bond losses and asset inflation, and gold funds took in $6.3 billion last week, the largest inflow since January.

Gold is Per the Financial Markets list, at $4,410 and has overtaken Treasuries as the top reserve asset, and the 30-year's blowout is the reason: a buyer who needs a store of value and a yield with no default risk is getting neither from the long end. The dollar is breaking down from 101 after crowded longs, and that breakdown is the leverage behind the metal. The McGlone counter is still live, that precious metals may have peaked against the 30-year, but the flows this week are on Hartnett's side and the house stays with the monetary bid.

3. Japan is the next rate shoe and the yen is the fault line. Japan's 10-year surged to 2.93 percent, the highest since 1996, with swaps pricing roughly an 80 percent chance of a September Bank of Japan hike. That is the carry trade unwinding again, and the yen rescue puts Asia currencies on notice. The standing house read holds: with the Exchange Stabilization Fund spent, the next yen defence needs the BOJ itself. If Tokyo hikes into an already-blowing global long end, the cross-rates pressure finds the dollar next. This is the leverage crack the Fed minutes will not cover but the market is already pricing.

4. Oil holds Hormuz and the deal clock ran out. The 60-day Iran deal deadline has passed with traffic through the strait still disrupted, and Trump is threatening to bomb Oman over the blockade while insisting he is "not in a hurry." Kalshi's odds that Hormuz traffic returns to normal keep falling, to 13 percent by March 2027. The energy complex already shows it: Per the Market list, European natural gas is up 117 percent this year, heating oil 100 percent, gasoline 86 percent, Brent 45 percent, US crude 43 percent. Oil above $170 in Asia is the swing variable into the week, and the gap between the Oman plan and the next attack is still a two-way open.

5. What to watch: the minutes, and whether the long end breaks the dollar. The Fed minutes land Wednesday, and they are the week's test of whether Warsh is the one moving the 30-year. But the higher-signal watch is the dollar: DXY is rolling over from 101 on crowded longs at the same time the long end blows out. That combination, a weaker dollar against a selling bond market, is the debasement trade confirming itself, and it is why gold, not bonds, is the asset catching the bid. The next catalyst after the minutes is Japan, and a BOJ hike into this long end would be the first real test the tape has not priced.

Fed minutes Wednesday, then Japan. If the long end keeps selling into data that should have helped it, that is the regime change, and the dollar is the casualty the books have not yet hedged.
