The rate-hike trade just broke, and the S&P ran straight through 7,800

September hike odds collapsed from 42.1 to 29 percent in one session on a hold call from Oxford Economics that said the Fed may not move for the rest of the year.

RatesGeopoliticsCommoditiesLiquidity

Yesterday the market still had a September hike at 42.1 percent chances after a CPI that landed on the estimate to the decimal. Today the floor fell out.

Market context. The S&P 500 broke above 7,800 for the first time ever, an intraday record, and the VIX touched 14.39, a new 2026 low. The session ran on energy: a Houthi attack on the Aramco refinery sent diesel cracks through $98 a barrel for an all-time high, gold futures held above $4,500 for the first time since June 5, and the yen inched back toward 160 per dollar. The 30-year auctioned at 5.21 percent on Tuesday and has not come in. Volume was the lowest in thirteen sessions for the S&P, which has now printed 26 all-time highs in 2026.

1. The hike odds broke, and the pivot reversed faster than it built. On Tuesday the market priced a September hike at 48.4 percent. On Wednesday it was 42.1 after the CPI. Today it is 29, and the hold stands at 70. Oxford Economics published a note saying the Fed is likely to hold for the rest of 2026, pointing to PCE inflation of 3.6 percent year over year in July against 3.7 in June and a labour market that is weakening but not breaking.

That is a fast trip. A month ago the September hike was at 69 percent. The jobs report took it to 48, the CPI nudged it to 42, and today a four-line forecast from a named shop took it to 29. The sequence is telling: a single payroll print, a single house call, and a single refinery attack have now dismantled more than 40 points of hike pricing in nine days. One more print, one more hawk to retreat, and the hold is a consensus.

The dissent is thin and it is not about the data. Warren Pies is still at a hike at 38 percent, arguing that the committee is under political pressure from a persistently missed inflation target, and he names Beth Hammack directly. Hammack herself said today that raising rates is painful but the Fed must not let growth and investment overheat. She did not say September and she did not say hold. The other hawk, Warsh, was quiet today.

What makes the pivot faster than the build is that the tail is pricing the same thing the front is. The December hike fell from 73.8 to 62.3. The October hike moved from 62.2 to 48.6. Any hike in 2026 is now at 47 percent, down from above 75 two weeks ago. When the back end of the year moves more than the front, the market is not just pricing a delay. It is pricing a reversal of the call. Goldman Sachs already declared the hold on Wednesday and nobody has stepped in front of them.

2. Iran is now asserting control over the strait and the oil market noticed. Iran said today that no vessel can transit the Strait of Hormuz without its permission. It reorganized its military to be more aggressive abroad, the State Department told embassies to draw up reduced-staff plans on fears of a return to war, and the US is sending a fresh carrier group led by the USS George Washington. The Reaper drone fleet is down about a quarter, with 45 MQ-9s lost at a replacement cost above $1.3 billion. The Pentagon is seeking $67 billion to replenish them.

The price is in diesel. Cracks broke above $98 a barrel, an all-time high, after the Houthis hit a Saudi Aramco refinery. A market that was oversupplied on paper at 106.3 million barrels a day of supply against 104.8 million of demand closed at a chokepoint it cannot control. Iran says it can keep the strait shut as long as necessary and that it is considering prolonging the war until Trump leaves office. Trump himself said the US has total control of the strait and thinks it will keep it.

Yesterday the Polymarket contract on the US announcing an end to the blockade by October 31 fell from 93 percent to 73. It has not moved back up today. The year-end contract sat at 81 cents.

3. Three firms reported after the bell and the after-hours trade is saying what the session trade already told you. Coherent beat, guided above the street, and fell six percent. Cerebras beat and fell fourteen, because operating expense came in at $500 million against $80 million expected and at a $60 billion market cap on less than a billion dollars of revenue, the valuation math matters the moment the numbers are not perfect. Cisco beat on networking products up 40 percent year over year and a Q1 guide more than a billion above the street, and still faded to red after hours.

The pattern is the same one the morning held: the S&P rallied 0.6 percent to a record and the headliners inside it, the $NVDA and the $NBIS and the semis, did the running, while the rest of the index held still. The equal-weight S&P is up fourteen of the last sixteen months. The cap-weight index is rallying because the megacaps are rallying, and the after-hours earnings are selectively punishing the ones whose numbers were not clean enough.

4. The bond market is doing its own thing and the yen is the link. The thirty-year auctioned at 5.21 percent on Tuesday, the highest in a quarter century, and the yield has barely moved from there. Japan's two-year hit 1.64 percent, the first time since 1995, while the yen pushed back toward 160 per dollar. At 160 the intervention question returns, because that is the level that triggered the first coordinated US-Japan intervention since 1998 last month alongside the Bessent FIMA repo proposal.

CreditSights published a note today saying the Fed could hold into 2027. If the long end is at 5.21 and the front end is holding, that is the steepener trade the rate-cut trade was built on. The yen at 160 and the thirty-year at 5.21 are two sides of a funding question, and the fuel on both sides is the deficit. The national debt hit a record $39.94 trillion today, this fiscal year's deficit is already larger than all of last year's, and $1.1 trillion a year in interest is the floor under the long end regardless of what the Fed does with the overnight rate.
