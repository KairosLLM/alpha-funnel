Warsh opened the door to a September hike, and Trump has been calling him

The Chair says he will raise if inflation runs hot, Kalshi prices a hike this year at 65 percent, and Treasury needs $739 billion of fresh borrowing in the same quarter.

RatesDollarPolicyGeopolitics

Yesterday the hawk was a regional president nobody has to obey. Today it is the Chair, and the man who appointed him has been on the phone since May.

Market context. Per the Market list, the Financial Times reports Kevin Warsh is prepared to raise rates in September if inflation prints run hot and markets price in higher borrowing costs. The data underneath him came in firm.

The dollar has not caught up to any of it. MUFG has the dollar index near a seven-week low and still undervalued against eight of the nine other G10 currencies, and Brent came back under $80. Payrolls land tomorrow. CPI on August 12 is the print Warsh named.

1. Warsh's condition has two legs, and the second one is a mirror. Per the Market list, people close to Warsh say he has acknowledged "early communication mistakes that blurred his message on price stability and Fed reform."

He named two triggers. Hot inflation prints, and markets pricing in higher borrowing costs. The second is the market itself, so a market that prices a hike is helping satisfy the condition for one.

The rest of the committee arrived in the same week. Per the Kalshi list, Governor Lisa Cook said she is ready to raise if inflation stays high and Kashkari said it is "slowly" time to raise. Invesco is the lone dissent that matters, and it argues about whether policy is already restrictive rather than about direction.

Then the part that is not economics. Per the Financial Markets list, the Wall Street Journal reports Trump held multiple undisclosed calls with Warsh, and Bloomberg dates them to May. A Chair turning hawkish while taking private calls from a President who wants cheap money is the least comfortable sequence available, and it argues the hawkishness is real.

2. Treasury needs $739 billion this quarter and the interest line already passed defense. Per ClearValue Tax, the August 3 Treasury statement guides to $739 billion of privately held net marketable borrowing for July through September, meaning debt sold to real buyers rather than shuffled between government accounts. That is $68 billion above the May estimate on less cash flow than projected. October through December is guided at $628 billion, $1.36 trillion across the back half.

That last row is where sections one and two meet. A third of the stock reprices inside a year, so a September hike is not a 2027 problem for the fiscal path. It is a 2026 one. Lyn Alden, on the Stocks list, puts it blunter: for a reserve currency adding $2 trillion a year, the exit is debasement rather than default.

3. Washington is defending the yen with euros, and nobody has seen that before. Per Prof G Markets, the US is intervening in the yen market using euros, and their guest is unambiguous about how unusual that is: "I have never heard of any country intervening in anyone else's market using a third party currency before."

The mechanism is mundane. The US reserve pot is held predominantly in euros and yen, so euros are what there is to sell. The consequence is not. "The US authorities have been in touch with the European Central Bank," and selling euros in size against the yen puts the distortion in a third currency belonging to a central bank that did not ask for any of this.

The failure underneath explains why it got this exotic. Per Trader Mayne, Japan has spent $160 billion this year, including $53 billion and $34 billion on consecutive days last week, and the yen firms for a few days and then goes right back down. His conclusion is that reserves cannot fix it and only rate hikes can. Handle his next step carefully, because he sources it to an AI explainer rather than a report: a forced Bank of Japan hike unwinds the yen carry trade (borrowing cheap yen to buy higher-yielding assets abroad), and Japan sells Treasuries into it.

I am not putting odds on that chain. The near end carries enough. Two sources now describe the US financing a foreign currency defense while its own Chair talks about tightening, and MUFG names that intervention as a reason the dollar sits at a seven-week low. The softness is partly a policy choice executed in somebody else's money.

4. CLARITY needs 60 votes, has 53, and nobody filed the paperwork. Per Milk Road, the bill cleared the House 294 to 134 last July and Senate Banking 15 to 9 in May. On the floor it needs 60. Republicans hold 53.

The mechanics killed it before the politics got a turn. Per VirtualBacon, cloture (the motion that ends debate and forces a vote) had to be filed today to ripen in time for a Friday vote, and Majority Leader John Thune filed cloture on a different motion instead. Punchbowl reports Democrats settled on voting no as a bloc unless Republicans move on ethics, illicit finance and stablecoin yield.

A committee arguing about a hike does not clear floor time for market-structure legislation, and 20 percent for the whole year says the market has worked that out.
