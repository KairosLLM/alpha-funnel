Two banks read the same payroll print and landed 75 basis points apart

Bank of America still wants three hikes starting in September, Morgan Stanley says none this year, and Wednesday's CPI decides which desk was reading the right number.

RatesInflationGeopoliticsPolicy

Yesterday a negative payroll print killed the September hike in a morning. It did not kill the argument. Bank of America read the same number and kept three hikes.

The week ahead. Wednesday's CPI is the only print that matters, because it is the one Warsh named as his trigger. Friday's labour data set the table.

Rate-hike probabilities fell roughly 20 points on the day. Gold took the week, finishing almost 8 percent higher, its biggest weekly gain since January per the Financial Markets list and more than the Nasdaq and S&P tracking funds combined.

1. The desks are further apart after the print than they were before it. Per the August 7 Market list, Bank of America stuck with 75 basis points of hikes this year starting in September, explicitly after payrolls fell 23,000 with 103,000 of downward revisions. Its stated reason is that inflation remains the Fed's primary concern.

Against that, per the Polymarket list, Morgan Stanley's chief strategist declared there will be "no rate hikes" this year.

That is a 75 basis point gap on the same data, the widest this product has recorded between two large desks all year. Both cannot be roughly right.

Goldman supplied the frame that decides it: inflation now matters more than jobs for the Fed. If that is the reaction function, then a labour market cracking is not a reason to hold, it is background, and Wednesday's CPI is the entire question. The market has not fully picked a side either. Per Anthony Pompliano's guest Jordi Visser, there is still a 44 percent chance of a September hike priced, with more than one tightening built in before year end.

My lean stays where I moved it Friday. 65/35 for a September hold. The reason is not the labour data, it is that Warsh named a conditional and the condition has to be met by a print rather than by an argument. But I am less comfortable at 65 than I was, because Bank of America is not a fringe desk and it looked at the same jobs report I did and did not blink.

2. China is dumping AI the way it dumped steel, and one of the analogies is worse than the other. Per Prof G Markets, tokens consumed from Chinese large language models went from 8 percent share in January 2025 to somewhere above 50 percent now. The framing offered is deliberate: China is "trying to do to our market what they tried to do to our steel market 30 or 40 years ago and doing in kind of 20 weeks to Silicon Valley what Japan did to Detroit over 20 years."

Twenty weeks against twenty years is the part to sit with. Trade dumping normally gives the incumbent time to lobby for tariffs. This did not.

The pricing evidence underneath is specific. Anthropic's cost to deliver what it charges $6,000 an hour for is not a software cost structure. Per the same show, the two American labs have already split on the response, with Anthropic going premium and OpenAI going for volume at lower cost, "and the Chinese are of course waiting on the sidelines to throw the story into complete turmoil."

The capex spread is the trade this creates. Everyone is spending $150 billion to $200 billion while Apple spends $11 billion, and the verdict offered is that this becomes a case study in whether it is better to wait out the uncertainty and then decide what factory to build, rather than build the factory first. This is the driver. What it does to the semis chain and to the names carrying the capex belongs to Stocks.

3. Japan has to pick how it gets wrecked, and the long end is already voting. Per Stocks with Josh, Japan holds over $1 trillion of US Treasuries, and the trap is that the Bank of Japan cannot sell enough of them to defend the yen without breaking the US bond market that funds American deficits. The choice as put: raise rates and blow up the domestic debt market, or cut and kill the currency. "There's no exit. They just simply have to pick how Japan gets wrecked."

This is the same thread that produced Thursday's confirmation that Treasury emptied its Exchange Stabilization Fund defending the yen, selling euros because euros were what remained. Visser adds the detail that dates it: this was the first coordinated US and Bank of Japan intervention since 1998, against 30-year yields at 20-year highs. His read is blunt. "The yen intervention is a representation that we have no more tools left."

The long end has already priced some of it. The main long-duration Treasury fund is pushing all-time lows since it launched in July 2002, yielding 5.14 percent, with a put-to-call skew near 7 to 1 and a short float around 20 percent. Per a widely-read breakdown, its historic drawdown dates read as a list of macro accidents, and the author adds the current one by name: 2007, 2013, 2018, 2020, 2023, 2025, and "August 2026, Kevin Warsh."

Being at an all-time low is not the same as being cheap. It is what a market looks like when it believes the supply and the policy both get worse. The position belongs to Stocks.

4. The Hormuz deal is close enough that the market has stopped paying for it. Per the August 7 Market list, Trump said the deal to reopen the strait is "moving along good" and stocks bounced on it. That is the seventh time this week he has said he wants an agreement. Bessent floated a ceasefire announcement over the weekend, and per the Polymarket list a US official set the condition plainly: the blockade of Iranian ports lifts once a deal restoring unimpeded commercial shipping is announced.

My 70/30 on a joint Iran-Oman announcement runs to Tuesday and is still live. What has changed is that the payoff has shrunk, because the tape now moves a few points on each headline instead of repricing crude.

The physical picture argues the same way. Global commercial crude inventories sit more than 40 million barrels higher than a year ago, and Anas Alhajji's read is the useful correction: the oil crisis is in petroleum products, not in crude. That is consistent with US pump prices setting a record $4.06 a gallon on August 4 while inventories build.

Two things sit against a clean resolution. ADNOC has logged 15 vessel attacks as the risk mounts, and Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan signed a defence agreement in Mecca on Friday. All three border Iran, and none of them signs that document if they expect the strait to be quietly settled next week.

So the week has one question and one date. If Wednesday's CPI runs hot, Bank of America is right and the September hike comes back with the labour market already cracking underneath it. If it does not, the hike is dead until the December meeting and everything above resolves toward the doves.
