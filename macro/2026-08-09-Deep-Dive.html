The September hike was born on Tuesday and died on Friday

Kashkari wanted it immediately, Warsh attached a condition, a negative payroll print destroyed the premise, and Bank of America still wants three hikes starting next month.

RatesInflationPolicyLiquidity

In five sessions this channel moved its own September call three times. That is either a system with no spine or a week in which the underlying facts genuinely reversed twice. It was the second one, and the reason is worth an entire memo, because the same sequence is about to repeat on Wednesday.

The chronology, because the order is the argument

Three days of hawkish escalation, one data release, and a 75 basis point disagreement between two of the largest desks on the street. Nothing about the reaction function changed. What changed is that one side is weighting a survey and the other is weighting a payroll.

1. Warsh's condition has two legs, and the second one is a mirror

The Chair did not say he would hike. Per the Market list, he said he is prepared to raise rates in September if inflation prints run hot and markets price in higher borrowing costs.

Read the second clause again. The market pricing a hike is itself part of the trigger for one. That is a reaction function with a feedback loop inside it, and it means positioning is not merely predicting the decision, it is an input to it. When Kalshi went to 65 percent on Thursday, that number was not just a forecast. It was evidence toward Warsh's own condition.

Which is why Friday mattered more than it should have. Hike odds fell from 60 percent to 30 inside a morning. That did not only reprice the probability, it removed one of the two legs the Chair named.

The context underneath is the part nobody enjoys. The Wall Street Journal reported Trump held multiple undisclosed calls with Warsh, dated by Bloomberg to May. A Chair turning hawkish while taking private calls from a President who wants cheap money is the least comfortable possible sequence. It also argues the hawkishness is genuine, because political pressure runs the other way.

2. What the payroll print actually broke

Per the Polymarket list, the headline was minus 23,000 against an expected gain of 80,000. The revisions were worse: May and June cut by a combined 103,000.

A single negative month is noise. A negative month plus 103,000 taken off the two before it is a trend being discovered late, and it means the Fed spent three weeks arguing about tightening into a labour market that was already softer than the data on its desk.

The rest of the release complicates it rather than settling it.

Unemployment falling to a one-year low in the same release that shows job losses is not a contradiction. It is what happens when people leave the labour force rather than lose jobs, and the St Louis Fed flagged prime-age participation as the line to read in this exact report before it landed.

So the hawkish case did not lose an argument. It lost its premise. You cannot run "the labour market is too tight" as a reason to hike when payrolls are negative and participation is falling.

3. Who is on each side, and the honest read of the gap

Per the August 7 Market list, Bank of America kept 75 basis points of hikes this year starting in September, explicitly after the print, on the stated reason that inflation remains the Fed's primary concern. Morgan Stanley's chief strategist says there will be no rate hikes this year.

That is the widest spread this channel has recorded between two large desks. Both cannot be roughly right.

Per the Polymarket list, Goldman supplied the frame that decides it: inflation now matters more than jobs for the Fed. If that is the reaction function, a cracking labour market is background rather than a reason to hold, and Wednesday's CPI is the whole question. The market has not fully picked a side either, with a 44 percent September hike still priced and more than one tightening built in before year end.

The dissent that deserves more weight than it gets is Invesco's, arguing the Fed is not behind the curve at all, with neutral at 3.5 to 4.0 percent against a current 3.50 to 3.75 range. If that is right, both the hikers and the cutters are solving a problem that does not exist.

4. The thing this week actually settled, which was not the Fed

While the rate argument went in circles, the funding story underneath it resolved. Joseph Wang, on the Financial Markets list: "Treasury has now spent all the ammo in their Exchange Stabilization Fund. They sold Euros to buy Yen because that's all they have in the ESF."

That confirmed something this channel had been calling a novel mechanism and reframed it as something worse. It was never a clever cross-currency strategy. Treasury sold euros because euros were what remained. Jordi Visser dates the action as the first coordinated US and Bank of Japan intervention since 1998, against 30-year yields at 20-year highs.

An empty stabilisation fund means the next yen defence needs either a Bank of Japan hike or Japan selling from its trillion dollars of Treasuries. Japan's own choice, as put plainly this week: raise rates and blow up the domestic debt market, or cut and kill the currency. There is no exit, only a decision about how it gets wrecked.

Set that beside Treasury guiding to $739 billion of privately held net marketable borrowing this quarter, $68 billion above the May estimate, with interest expense already running $827 billion through June against $749 billion a year earlier and 33 percent of the debt maturing inside twelve months. A September hike is not a 2027 problem for the fiscal path. It is a 2026 one.

What settles it, and what would change my mind

Wednesday, August 12, CPI. This is the print Warsh named. A hot core reading restores the first leg of his condition and Bank of America is right with a labour market cracking underneath. A soft one kills September and probably the year.

Tuesday, September 16, the FOMC. Between now and then the only other thing that matters is whether the payroll revisions continue, because two consecutive months of downward revisions stop being a data artifact and start being a recession signal.

I am at 65/35 for a September hold, above where this call started the week. I am less comfortable at 65 than the number suggests, for one reason: Bank of America is not a fringe desk, it looked at the same jobs report I did, and it did not blink. What would move me back toward a hike is a core CPI print above expectations combined with the market re-pricing the odds itself, because that combination satisfies both of Warsh's legs at once and he has told us in advance that it would.

What would move me further toward a hold is a third consecutive month of downward payroll revisions. That would make Friday a trend rather than an event, and no committee tightens into that.
