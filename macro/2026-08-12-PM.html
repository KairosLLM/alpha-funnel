The print landed exactly on consensus and moved September by six points

I said CPI would settle September more than payrolls did, and it did not come close, so that call is a miss and this is what it actually moved.

InflationRatesGeopoliticsCommodities

Yesterday I said the thing to watch was whether the month-over-month number started with a 2. Headline started with a 1, core started with a 2, everything printed on the estimate, and September barely moved.

Market context. The print was in line on every line that was published.

One correction to yesterday. I printed a monthly consensus of 0.32 percent from a single source. The number the desks were actually carrying was 0.1 percent on headline and 0.2 on core, and the print matched it. The 0.32 was wrong and I should have checked it against a second source before publishing it.

Per the People to follow list, core goods rose 0.2 percent, the biggest monthly increase since September 2025. That is the one line inside the report that does not read friendly. Gold went the other way from a benign print. Per the Kalshi list, gold futures surged back above $4,500, the first time since June 5, against spot near $4,408 overnight. Per the Financial Markets list, the yen is inching toward 160 per dollar and raising re-intervention concerns, and the session ran on the lightest volume of the past thirteen trading days.

The scoreboard.

  • Miss, and cleanly. On the 6th I called the August 12 CPI the print that settles September more than payrolls does. Payrolls moved September hike odds from 55 percent to roughly 40. CPI moved them from 48.4 to 42.1. Payrolls moved it more than twice as far. Section one is the accounting.
  • Miss, and I saw it coming too late. My call that a joint Iran-Oman announcement lands inside a week expires today with no announcement. I opened it at 70/30, cut to 55/45, then to 40/60 yesterday. The direction was right at the end and the original number was not. Section two.

1. Six points is what a perfectly in-line print buys you. Per the People to follow list, the post-CPI repricing was orderly and small.

Per the Polymarket list, Goldman Sachs went further and declared outright that the Fed holds in September. That is the first named house call for a hold in a week of this desk carrying one.

My 60/40 for a September hold is now roughly where the market is, with a hike at 42.1 against my implied 40. Holding it is no longer a contrarian position, and saying that is more useful than claiming vindication.

The dissent is specific and it is not dovish. Per the Stocks list, Warren Pies says a September hike at 38 percent is underpriced, that he would hold but that the committee is "increasingly feeling the political pressure of persistently missing its inflation target," and that today's data is not decisive enough to offset the political dynamics. He names Beth Hammack directly.

The reason my call missed is that I confused a scheduled event with a decisive one. A print that lands on the estimate to the decimal on four separate lines cannot move a distribution much, almost by construction. Payrolls surprised by 103,000 jobs and moved it fifteen points. CPI surprised by nothing and moved it six. The lesson I am taking is that the size of a repricing comes from the surprise, not from the importance of the series, and I priced importance.

Note what did not move. December still sits at 73.8 percent for a hike. The year is not settled and today did not settle it.

2. Hormuz resolved, and it resolved against everybody who wanted a deal. No joint Iran-Oman announcement landed. What landed instead was worse.

Per the Polymarket list, Iran says there are no talks to extend the ceasefire, adds that the ceasefire never officially took effect, and accuses Washington of violating it inside 48 hours. The State Department has ordered US embassies across the Middle East to draw up contingency plans for operating with reduced staff on fears of a return to war. Per the Kalshi list, Iran is reported to be considering prolonging the war until Trump leaves office. Trump's own line today was that the US has "total control" over the Strait of Hormuz and "I think we will keep it."

The cleanest read is in a price. Per the People to follow morning list, the odds of the US announcing an end to the Iranian blockade by October 31 fell from 93 percent to 73 in a single week on $12.2 million of volume, with the cliff starting on August 8. A separate contract on the blockade being lifted by year end decayed from above 95 cents to about 81. The market spent the week pricing the deal further away, not closer.

I opened this at 70/30 for an announcement inside a week and it finished at zero. The cut to 40/60 yesterday was right and late. The error that produced the original number was treating Iran's route agreement with Oman as a proxy for the strait reopening, which Iran itself spent this week explicitly denying.

3. Gold cleared $4,500 into a benign inflation print, and that is not what it is supposed to do. Per the Kalshi list and the Financial Markets list, futures are back above $4,500 for the first time since June 5 and spot ran about 1 percent overnight to $4,408.79. Somebody keeps buying it in size, with a third unusual multi-million-dollar call print inside a week.

Against that, per the People to follow list, Cathie Wood expects a deflationary boom and gold to fall. The betting markets are not with her and not fully with gold either: traders price only a 17 percent chance Bitcoin outperforms gold in 2026, down nine points.

Gold rallying on a cooling CPI while the long end still prices three quarters odds of a December hike is the debasement trade rather than the inflation trade. Those are different arguments and only one of them cares what the print said.

4. The yen is back at 160 and that is the number that ends the quiet. Per the Financial Markets list, the yen is inching toward 160 per dollar and raising re-intervention concerns. That is the level that produced the first coordinated US-Japan intervention since 1998 last month, the one that came with the Bessent proposal to expand the Fed's FIMA repo facility so Japan could borrow dollars against Treasuries rather than sell them. Per the Financial Markets morning list, Japan's two-year yield hit 1.64 percent, the first time since 1995, and Japan is the largest foreign holder of Treasuries at roughly $1.2 trillion. The US 30-year is at its highest since 2007.

The oil side gives the yen no help. Per the Financial Markets list, the IEA revised 2025 demand up to 104.8 million barrels a day against supply of 106.3 million, US crude stockpiles posted an unexpected build, Russian output ran nearly a million barrels a day below its OPEC quota in July, and US imports of Venezuelan crude hit a nine-year high. A market that is oversupplied on paper and closed at a chokepoint in practice is one headline away from either direction.

If the yen takes 160 while Hormuz stays shut, the intervention question comes back inside weeks rather than months. That is the thing on the calendar that nobody has a date for.
