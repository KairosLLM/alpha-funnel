The long end is now the story and it is taking everything with it

Ten-year yields crossed 4.7 percent, gold and silver fell on the move, and the Fed minutes land Wednesday as the test of the regime.

RatesInflationDollarCommoditiesGeopolitics

Yesterday the question was whether the long end could keep selling into data that should have helped it. Today it answered: ten-year yields crossed 4.7 percent, the 30-year sat at 5.3 percent, and for the first time this cycle the debasement hedges broke with it, gold down 1 percent and silver down 3 percent. The minutes are tomorrow and they are now the fulcrum of the whole tape.

Market context. The 30-year is at 5.3 percent, the highest since 2007, and the 10-year near 4.73 percent with Yardeni calling 5 percent the key line. Gold sits at $4,362.79, down 1 percent, silver at $63.72, down 3 percent, copper at a record above $6.77 a pound. Oil is above $91 with Trump saying there are no Iran talks and the Hormuz blockade stands. Pending home sales fell 2.3 percent in July against a 0.6 percent expectation. The Fed minutes land Wednesday.

The scoreboard. On the 28th I carried Anas Alhajji's call that the July 27 Abqaiq strike was inflated by recycled 2019 reporting, and it proved out: the strikes were real but far short of 2019, and the oil story has moved on to the Hormuz blockade with crude above $91. The week's due catalysts on this book are the Fed minutes Wednesday, the call that decides whether the 30-year at 5.3 percent is regime change or a pullback, and the oil call due Friday, with crude above $91 and Trump confirming he is not extending the Iran deal.

1. The long end is the driver and today it broke the hedges. The 30-year at 5.3 percent was the story, and the day's signal is what it did to the assets that were supposed to hedge it. Gold fell 1 percent to $4,362.79 and silver fell 3 percent to $63.72 as yields rose, the exact trade the house flagged when the 30-year kept selling into soft data. Yardeni has the line at 5 percent on the 10-year, with the range at 4 to 5 percent and anything above 5 percent the concern.

The mechanism underneath has not changed: US interest payments are now 3.3 percent of GDP, the highest level in history, and $1.4 trillion over the past year, nearly triple what they were in 2020. That is the funding cost the long end keeps pricing, and it is why the hedge trade is now the long end's victim rather than its escape.

2. The Fed minutes Wednesday decide whether this is regime change. The call the house has been building toward all week lands tomorrow. The minutes cover the decision that cut September hike odds toward 30 percent, and they are the test of whether Warsh is the one moving the 30-year. The market is already pricing the world the minutes have to confirm or deny: Polymarket sits at 76 percent no Fed change in September against 24 percent for a hike, down from 43 percent a week ago.

Benjamin Cowen reads the rate-cutting cycle as over, expects a 25-basis-point hike in September or December, and models a 10 to 20 percent stock correction as the base case that lets Bitcoin find its cycle bottom. The house does not fully sign onto the correction size, but the direction of his argument is the same one the long end is making: the last cut was 2025, and the data is now pushing the other way.

3. Japan is the next shoe and it got louder. Japan's 10-year sits near 2.95 percent, a 30-year high and a level unseen since 1996, and the 30-year has surged above 4 percent for the first time in history. The carry trade is the mechanism: borrow from Japan at 1 percent, put it in US Treasuries at 3.75 percent up to a little above 5 percent, and keep the spread, with Japan holding more than $1.1 trillion of US Treasuries. The carry unwind is the largest tail risk on the desk, and Capital Flows flags it alongside rate volatility as the two risks nobody is hedged for.

The yen rescue has already moved up the ladder: Japan holds more than $1.1 trillion of US Treasuries and the Fed's FIMA repo can lend up to $60 billion. The house read holds, that the next yen defence needs the BOJ itself, and a September hike into an already-blowing global long end is the cross-rate pressure that finds the dollar.

4. Oil holds Hormuz and the war risk is two-way. Trump says there are no Iran talks, the naval blockade stands, and Hormuz is open with all mines removed. Oil sits above $91 with the diesel crack at a record $102, and Monday's only green S&P sector was energy, rising more than $2 a barrel on the renewed tensions. The strategic reserve is at 293.4 million barrels, the lowest since 1982.

This is the swing variable into the Fed minutes. A de-escalation drops the inflation input and gives the long end a reason to catch a bid. A further strike pushes oil toward the $100 forecasts and confirms the regime. The two-way open the house flagged Friday is still open.

5. What to watch: the minutes, then the long end against gold. The minutes land Wednesday, and the higher-signal tell is whether gold holds with them. Gold down on the same day yields rise is the debasement trade getting tested, and that is the real question: does the long end keep breaking the non-yield assets, or does gold reassert as the reserve bid. Copper at a record says the inflation bid is alive even as the metal hedges sag. The minutes answer the rates half, the next oil print answers the commodity half, and the two together decide if this is regime change or a pullback.

Fed minutes Wednesday. The long end against gold is the trade the whole book is watching.
