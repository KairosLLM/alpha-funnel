The 30-year is at 5.3 percent and the bond market will not take the cuts

Long rates are the highest since 2007 even after 175 basis points of Fed cuts, debt is crossing $40 trillion, and oil is the lever into a heavy data week.

RatesInflationDollarGeopoliticsCommodities

Friday's close left the S&P at a record, but the driver underneath was the long end of the bond market, and it is not taking the Fed's medicine. This is a week-ahead read on the forces that own the tape, and the long bond is the strongest of them.

Market context. The 30-year Treasury yields about 5.3 percent, the highest since 2007, and it has done that against 175 basis points of cuts since September. The 10-year closed Friday around 4.7 percent. Oil is the wildcard into Monday: US pump prices are the highest ever for this point in August, the strategic reserve is at 1983 lows, and the strait that carries the world's crude is the one unresolved driver. The week hands over Fed minutes, the first PMIs, and CPI and jobs prints across Europe, Britain, Japan and Canada.

1. The long end is calling the bluff on the whole rate regime. The 30-year at Per the Financial Markets list, 5.3 percent with only about 42 basis points of hikes priced through 2027 is the tell. The long end is bear-steepening through a Fed debate, and that shape of curve is how a market says nominal growth is running hotter than the consensus believes. The same move is global: France's 30-year printed 4.85 percent, its highest since the global financial crisis, and Japan's two-year sits at 1.66 percent, the highest in 31 years.

The key is what did not happen. Inflation retraced its entire spike and bonds never caught a bid, so the pressure on the long end is structural, not cyclical, Per Capital Flows Research, with the S&P, the 30-year and the dollar starting to lock into the same alignment. The whole post-2020 trade, the 60/40 and the thirty-year-refi playbook, was built on a regime of ever-falling rates. That regime ended, and the industry has not repriced the habit.

2. The debt engine is compounding while the Fed runs QE. The July deficit printed at a record $432 billion, the Treasury is set to borrow $1.367 trillion across 182 days, and national debt is about to cross 40 trillion dollars against roughly 19 trillion a decade ago. Interest payments now exceed national defense spending, behind only Social Security and Medicare, and they are the fastest line in the budget. Tax revenue is up 65 percent to $5.3 trillion over ten years while spending is up 96 percent to $7.3 trillion, so the gap is structural, not a one-off.

The uncomfortable part is the Fed's role. It has cut rates and is expanding its balance sheet again while inflation has run above target for 65 straight months at about 4 percent a year since 2019. Easing into an inflation you cannot shake is how a central bank ends up choosing between the bond market and the dollar. That is the choice Warsh and Bessent are being handed this autumn, and the market is pricing for one side of it.

3. Oil is the week's real lever, and the signals point both ways. The bulls and the bears are both here. The bullish resolution: Iran confirmed an agreement with Oman on a new Hormuz shipping-traffic plan, the classic way a chokepoint trade unwinds. The escalation side: Bessent says Washington is readying an economic isolation of Iran the world has never seen, the UAE accuses Iran of a third attack on its ADNOC facilities, and Trump keeps threatening to declare Hormuz US territory. Only two vessels crossed the strait Friday and neither carried crude.

The physical market is already paying for the risk. Oil traded above $170 a barrel in Asia, US gas is $4.07 a gallon, the highest ever for this point in August, and the strategic petroleum reserve is at its lowest since 1983 after a 52 percent drawdown in five years. Last week's claim that the reroute was priced was premature. This is the single largest swing variable into Monday, and the difference between the Oman plan and the next attack is a gap in either direction.

4. The first cracks in the leverage are showing, just under the record. Jane Street, one of the largest market makers in the world, lost roughly $15 billion in July, its first red trading month since 2016, on exposure to the Aschenbrenner book that collapsed a month ago. Net trading revenue is still up about $40 billion for the year, so this is not a solvency story. It is a warning about how concentrated the unwind can get when a single momentum book turns, and it is worth holding next to the record equity valuations that rely on that leverage staying calm.

What I am watching this week: Fed minutes, the first August PMIs, and the CPI and jobs prints out of Europe, Britain, Japan and Canada, plus the White House crypto meeting on Wednesday. The bond market is the driver and oil is the swing. If the long end keeps selling into prints that should have helped it, that is the signal the regime change is real, not a pullback.
