Iran and Oman agreed the Hormuz route, and gold rose four percent anyway

The strait is being reopened by the mediator nobody named, Kashkari wants a hike immediately, and the Street is now split six ways on September with Kalshi at a coin flip.

GeopoliticsRatesCommoditiesInflation

Yesterday Bessent said a Hormuz deal might land today or tomorrow and pointed at Qatari draft text. The deal is landing. The mediator turned out to be Oman, and gold is behaving as though none of it happened.

Market context. Per the Market list, Iran's foreign ministry confirmed three things in the space of two minutes: the geographic characteristics of the route through the Strait of Hormuz have been agreed by Iran and Oman, a joint announcement is in its final stage if "certain third parties" do not interfere, and the agreement "in itself" does not mean the strait is secure.

The rates picture moved further than the geopolitics did.

The scoreboard.

  • Working, and through the wrong door. I went 60/40 yesterday that a Hormuz agreement gets announced inside two weeks. The route is agreed and a joint announcement is in its final stage. I had the channel wrong, because Qatar's draft text was not the one that closed it.
  • Miss, and I am calling it now rather than waiting for the date. On August 2 I went 65/35 that oil would be higher two weeks out. Three sessions later there is a signed route and the risk premium is gone. The call runs to August 16 and it is not coming back.
  • Due Friday. Payrolls, which everything this week routes through.

1. Oman closed it, and the hedge in Iran's own statement is the part to read. The sequence matters because it tells you who actually has leverage. Bessent named Qatar yesterday and put a 24-hour clock on it. What arrived instead was an Iran-Oman route agreement, with Tehran adding two qualifications in the same breath.

The first is procedural: the joint announcement happens if "certain third parties" do not interfere. That is a pre-built escape hatch, and it is aimed at Washington and Jerusalem.

The second is substantive and more important. An agreed route is not a secure strait. Iran is conceding transit geography while explicitly reserving the right to treat passage as conditional. That is the same permissioned-corridor structure it described on Sunday, now with a signature attached.

So the reopening is real and the de-risking is partial. Per the Market list, Russia hit three cargo vessels in the Black Sea the same day. Shipping risk is not a single-theatre story.

I am 70/30 that a joint announcement is made inside a week, up from 60/40 on the route agreement. The 30 is the third-party clause, and the reason I am not higher is that Iran has now written its own permission to walk away into the text.

2. Kashkari wants a hike now, and the Street cannot agree on anything. Per the Market list, Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari said the Fed should begin raising rates now, backing 25 basis points and warning that delay could force more aggressive tightening if inflation becomes entrenched. He was one of the three who dissented last week.

Then look at how far apart the desks are on the same meeting.

BofA's own chief executive Brian Moynihan reiterated the three-hike forecast publicly, saying the labour market is strong while inflation still needs to ease. Citi is at the opposite pole with three cuts. Those cannot both be roughly right.

Janet Yellen supplied the frame that fits the data best. The labour market is functioning well and no longer looks hot enough to fuel inflation, and she is surprised the Middle East conflict and higher oil prices have had such limited economic impact. Her conclusion is that the challenge is still bringing inflation down without harming the economy.

My lean. I am 55/45 that September is a hold, down from the near-certainty of a month ago, and the reason is Kashkari rather than the data. Three dissents plus a regional president publicly campaigning for immediate action is how a committee talks itself into moving. The cleanest read is that the hike is a question of timing, not direction, and Citi's cut scenario requires something to break that has not broken.

3. Goldilocks arrived and the pipeline warning did not clear. Per Milk Road, the case is genuinely strong. ISM at 55.6 is the best since May 2022 and the seventh consecutive month of expansion. S&P 500 net profit margins hit 16.7 percent, the highest FactSet has recorded since it began tracking in 2009. And inflation cooled in the latest reading after several months of increases, partly because easing Iran tensions took energy prices down.

Growth accelerating while inflation falls is the setup that makes everything work, and it is why the equity tape looks the way it does.

Two things sit against it and both are dated. ISM Prices Paid is still at 71.1, elevated on tariff-driven input costs, and that has not passed through yet. And the breadth measure that held the same index at caution yesterday has not recovered.

Which is why Friday matters more than any of it. A payroll print inside consensus puts a labour market under the ISM surge and the Goldilocks read holds. A second consecutive miss near June's 57,000 leaves a strong manufacturing survey next to a weakening jobs market, and that combination has historically resolved toward the jobs data rather than the survey.

4. Gold is up four percent and is not supposed to be. Per the Crypto list, gold futures extended gains to 4 percent on the day and rose above $4,300 an ounce, with the metal running from roughly $370 to almost $392 on the tracking fund in two sessions.

Read that against everything above. The war premium is leaving oil, the strait is reopening, equities are at records and margins are the best since 2009. Gold should have nothing to do.

Instead it is the strongest thing on the board, which says the bid is not geopolitical. It is either the rate path, where a committee arguing about immediate hikes while a Treasury Secretary asks for a Fed facility to be enlarged is exactly the institutional picture gold responds to, or it is currency, with the same Treasury endorsing a stronger yen days after intervening to get one.

Mike McGlone's line is the useful counterweight and it belongs here: copper, gold and Bitcoin are all sock puppets to the S&P 500 right now. If he is right, gold is simply levered risk appetite and carries no signal at all. I lean against him at 60/40, because equities and gold have now risen together for two sessions while oil fell, and that combination is a monetary story rather than a risk-appetite one.
