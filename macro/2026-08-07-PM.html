Payrolls lost 23,000 jobs and the September hike died in a morning

May and June were revised down a combined 103,000, Kalshi's hike odds fell from 60 percent to 30, and the S&P printed a record high on the news.

RatesPolicyGeopoliticsDollar

Yesterday I reversed and said the Fed leans toward hiking in September. This morning's print took eighteen hours to make that look silly.

Market context. Per the Polymarket list, July nonfarm payrolls shed 23,000 jobs against expectations for an 80,000 gain, and May and June were revised down by a combined 103,000. The revisions are the worse half.

Per the Kalshi list, unemployment hit a one-year low even as the payroll line went negative, and traders had been forecasting 4.2 percent. The S&P printed a new all-time high of 7,740 ninety minutes after the number. Gold futures went the other way and cleared $4,400, up 8.1 percent in 72 hours. CPI on August 12 is still the print Warsh named.

The scoreboard.

  • Wrong inside eighteen hours, and I am reverting. Yesterday I moved to 45/55 against a September hold on the strength of Warsh, Cook and Kashkari all turning hawkish in two days. A negative payroll print outranks three speeches. Back to 65/35 for a hold, above where I started.
  • Confirmed, and by the plumbing itself. I said Washington was defending the yen with euros. Joseph Wang, on the Financial Markets list: "Treasury has now spent all the ammo in their Exchange Stabilization Fund. They sold Euros to buy Yen because that's all they have in the ESF."
  • Resolved. Payrolls were the catalyst I flagged for today. Kathy Lien's "stronger payrolls, but probably not a blowout" missed by 103,000.
  • Due Wednesday. CPI, which now has to do all the work.

1. The revisions matter more than the headline, and the unemployment rate is lying. A single negative month is noise. A negative month plus 103,000 taken off the two before it is a trend being discovered late. That is the shape of a labour market that was already softer than the data said while the Fed was arguing about tightening into it.

The unemployment rate falling to a one-year low in the same release looks like a contradiction and is not. It is what happens when people leave the labour force rather than lose jobs. Per the Financial Markets list, the St Louis Fed flagged prime-age participation as the line to read in this exact report, noting more than half of the recent decline came from a January population correction and from ageing rather than from workers quitting.

June JOLTS pointed the same way before anyone was watching: openings down 178,000 to 7.359 million against 7.454 million expected, with the openings-to-unemployed ratio at 1.04.

So the hawkish case did not lose an argument. It lost its premise.

2. The Exchange Stabilization Fund is empty, and that closes the yen story. Joseph Wang's line above is the confirmation the euro-intervention story needed, and it reframes the whole thing. This was never a clever cross-currency strategy. Treasury sold euros because euros were what remained in the account.

An empty stabilisation fund means the next yen defence needs either the Bank of Japan raising rates or Japan selling reserves. Japan holds over a trillion dollars of Treasuries. Per the Crypto list, the ten-year sat at 4.73 percent with Fidelity's Jurrien Timmer calling anything above 4.5 percent the danger zone, and the warning attached is the one that matters: the Fed can cut and long yields can still rise.

Today's print pushes that day further out. It does not cancel it.

3. Bessent says he has Iran by the throat and the strait opens within days. Per the Polymarket list, the Treasury Secretary put a clock on it: "We have them by the throat, and they've got 150 to 180% food inflation, not able to pay the troops. I think shortly, maybe even today, tomorrow, we are going to see an agreement, a 30- to 60-day ceasefire, and the Strait will be open."

The enforcement numbers behind that leverage are real. Per the August 6 Polymarket list, CENTCOM has redirected 49 commercial vessels, disabled two and boarded two, and US imports of Saudi crude fell to zero in July, the first full month since 1985. Polymarket prices the blockade being lifted this month at 69 percent.

Tehran is not behaving like a government about to sign. An Iranian lawmaker has a preliminary bill barring US, Israeli and other hostile vessels from the strait with fines up to 20 percent of cargo value, and Trump's own read was "it's sort of open right now" with the caveat that "they can always shoot something."

Gold going up 8.1 percent in three days while a ceasefire is being announced tells you which one the money believes. I am 60/40 that a ceasefire is announced inside two weeks and under 50/50 that shipping volumes recover inside a month.

4. CLARITY is a September problem, and nobody filed the paperwork in time. Per the Financial Markets list, the Senate pushed the vote past summer recess. The mechanism was the one I named on Thursday: cloture was never filed. Eleanor Terrett had it live at the deadline, reporting worry on both sides that a failed cloture vote would bring the whole year-long effort down, and that if it was not filed that night it would not happen.

Elizabeth Warren supplied the durable objection, saying she supports crypto legislation but not this one, "written by the crypto industry to protect and advance the crypto industry." Thune ran out of floor time. Schumer wanted more talks.

Kalshi's odds it becomes law this year hit 16 percent, an all-time low. My 80/20 that it does not pass in 2026 is intact and I am raising it to 85/15, because the September calendar is already spoken for. The Senate comes back on the 14th with a government to fund and a Fed meeting two days later. Haseeb Qureshi is the useful counterweight, pointing out Kalshi still prices 30 percent plus for next year and above 50 percent by 2028. This bill is late, not dead, and the difference matters for anyone sizing a position on it.
