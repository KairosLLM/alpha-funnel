Iran is close on Oman and says that still does not reopen the strait

Tehran's price is now US withdrawal, war reparations and the blockade lifted, Polymarket has a deal by month end at 30 percent, and both crude benchmarks rose a dollar on the doubt.

GeopoliticsRatesCommoditiesDollar

Yesterday I said the Hormuz call was the one I was least comfortable with. Tehran spent the weekend proving the discomfort was right and the number was not.

Market context. Gold is the asset doing the arguing. Per the Financial Markets list, it is easing back from a seven-week high of $4,372 while the dollar recovers from its post-payrolls slump on renewed Hormuz risk. Per the Kalshi list, Kalshi's traders forecast $4,566 this year, about 4.4 percent above that high. Crude went the other way from the headlines, with Brent and WTI each up more than a dollar. Wednesday's CPI is the only event on the calendar that matters.

The scoreboard.

  • Due Wednesday, and I am cutting it. My 70/30 that a joint Iran-Oman announcement lands inside a week goes to 55/45. Section one is the reason.
  • Due Wednesday. The August 12 CPI print I called on the 6th as the thing that settles September more than payrolls did. That still looks right, and it is now carrying more weight than I wanted it to.

1. Iran got closer to Oman and further from reopening, and those are two different agreements. Per the Market list, Iran says it is "very close to an agreement with Oman" on a new maritime transit route, and in the same breath warns that an Oman deal would not mean the strait reopens.

That distinction is the whole trade, and I underweighted it. My call was written on the route agreement being real, and the route agreement is real. It just turns out to be the cheap half.

Tehran's stated price for the strait itself is now explicit and maximal.

| Iran's preconditions to reopen Hormuz | |---| | US forces withdrawn from the region | | The naval blockade lifted | | Sanctions removed and frozen assets released | | An end to proxy warfare | | War reparations | | An end to hostile rhetoric |

Against that list, per the Polymarket list, the market prices a US-Iran Hormuz agreement by the end of the month at 30 percent, and it moved to that from a higher projection. Per the Kalshi list, Iran rejected talks outright over the weekend.

The two governments are also describing different games. Trump told Axios the US is only "semi-negotiating" and that it is "low-keying it", with oil near $75 doing the work of cushioning the consumer while the blockade grinds. Araghchi says no talks are happening at all and cannot restart while Washington breaches the June interim agreement. Pezeshkian says now is the best time for a deal. Three positions, one government on each side, and only one of them is a negotiation.

I am cutting the announcement call to 55/45 rather than killing it, because the Oman route genuinely is close and that is what the call names. What I got wrong was treating the route as a proxy for the strait. It is not, and Iran said so itself.

2. The Fed question that nobody would put a number on today. Friday's payrolls settled nothing. Per Milk Road, July shed 23,000 jobs against an expected gain of 80,000, and unemployment still fell from 4.2 percent to 4.1 because 264,000 people left the labour force. Participation is 61.4 percent, the lowest since early 2021. A print that bad on the headline and that ambiguous underneath is why nobody moved.

What did move is the framing. The Fed risk in this feed is being discussed as a hike, not a cut, with the Street reading Asian currencies as buoyed by reduced rate-hike prospects. Funds sit at 3.50 to 3.75 percent and roughly one hike is still priced for 2026.

Milk Road's mechanism for that hike never arriving is the one specific thing on offer: Kevin Warsh has five task forces running on inflation, jobs, data and the balance sheet, and the expectation is that one returns hawkish and four return dovish, which hands him the cover to cut instead.

I am holding 60/40 for a September hold, unchanged from yesterday. Not one source in today's material published a September probability, and Jordi Visser, who had it at 45 percent, wrote three thousand words on Japan without restating it. When the desks that were arguing go quiet in the same week as the print, the print is the argument. Wednesday settles it.

3. The yen rescue got its plumbing explained, and the answer is the Fed's balance sheet. Per Jordi Visser, the intervention was the first coordinated US-Japan action on the yen since June 1998, and the fix for Japan's Treasury problem is not the currency leg at all. Bessent has publicly proposed the Fed expand the FIMA repo facility, with Treasury willing to raise the cap, so Japan can borrow dollars against its Treasury holdings rather than sell them.

Visser's read on that is the honest one. FIMA is not money printing, and it is "a verbal bazooka indicating they are scared." Watch what it produced: the largest monthly rise in the US 30-year yield since this administration took over, and the highest monthly yield close in more than twenty years.

One caution on the source. Visser describes this as a dollar-yen operation throughout and never mentions the euro leg, and he lists no conditions attached to the rescue. This desk has been carrying both. Treat his account as filling in the mechanism rather than as agreeing on the terms.

4. Gold is beating positive real rates, and the household is paying the bill. Lyn Alden's framing, carried in the same Milk Road letter, is that gold rallying against positive real rates is supposed to be impossible. Fiscal dominance is what makes it possible: with debt above 100 percent of GDP, raising rates blows out the federal deficit faster than it slows bank lending. The policy rate stopped being a brake and became a line item.

The cost of that shows up in one series. Per the Financial Markets list and the St. Louis Fed, the 30-year mortgage is 6.69 percent, the highest since July 2025, with the 15-year at 6.01.

That is the other end of the same rate: gold is the hedge against the deficit, and the mortgage and the private loan book are where the deficit gets paid for. Wednesday's print decides which of the two the Fed is allowed to care about.
