The debasement trade finally picked a leg, and it is gold, not Bitcoin

Gold is near $5,000 on the books while the dollar hits new lows and Bitcoin sits still, and the Fed minutes this week test whether the long end bends.

RatesInflationDollarCommoditiesGeopolitics

The long end was Friday's story and it is still the driver, but the weekend moved the other half of the trade. Gold is running while Bitcoin is not, and that split is the freshest signal this tape has. This is a week-ahead read on the forces that own it.

Market context. The 30-year holds near 5.3 percent, its highest since 2007, against 175 basis points of cuts. The dollar is at new lows while gold nears the $5,000 forecast the books carry. Oil trades above $170 in Asia and Iran still holds Hormuz. The week hands over Fed minutes, the first PMIs, and CPI and jobs prints across Europe, Britain, Japan and Canada, with the White House crypto meeting on Wednesday.

1. Gold is running the debasement trade and Bitcoin is still on the bench. Kalshi traders forecast gold near $5,000 this year, and the flows underneath agree. China added 20 tons of gold in July, its largest monthly purchase since October 2023, and the Bank of Korea resumed buying after 13 years. The dollar is already at new lows and silver has moved. The one thing that has not moved is Bitcoin, and that divergence is the whole story. The debasement bid is real and it is not waiting for crypto to catch up. When the asset that used to be the hedge stays flat while the metals run, the buyers are rotating away from it.

2. The Fed minutes land this week, and the hike odds already collapsed. The week's rate catalyst is the minutes, and the pricing has moved before it. Per Visser Labs, September is down to 31 percent and the BOJ sits at 80 percent, from roughly even before payrolls. Warsh implied the PCE index could be jettisoned as the Fed's primary inflation metric as soon as January and speaks at Jackson Hole. The 30-year at 5.3 percent is the market's vote that nominal growth is running hotter than the cuts suggest. The minutes will not decide the regime, but they will tell us whether Warsh is the one moving it.

3. Oil holds Hormuz and the producers are printing cash. Oil above $170 in Asia is still the swing variable, and the drivers have not changed. Trump keeps threatening to declare the strait US territory while Iran holds the chokepoint, and Kalshi still puts only a 40 percent chance traffic returns to normal this year. The other side is big oil's record $70 billion of free cash flow in Q2, with the UAE glutting the market near 4.1 million barrels a day, and Cathie Wood calling the inflation pressure a deflation echo. The bull and the bear are both priced, and the difference between the Oman deal and the next attack is still a gap in either direction.

4. The leverage cracks keep widening just under the record. Jane Street lost more than $15 billion in July, its first losing month since 2016, and Nomura flags a $300 billion coiled spring of exotic exposure. A record Per the Kalshi list, 200 leveraged ETFs launched in 2025 against fewer than 50 in 2024, and retail bought $27 billion of Nvidia over the past year while selling $5 billion of Apple. None of this is a solvency story yet. It is the same warning as the long end: the leverage that carried the rally is the part that can turn first.

5. What to watch: the trade map and the debt engine. The US import map keeps reordering itself, with Per the People to follow list, China falling from the number one source at 20.2 percent to number four at 7.4 percent while Mexico takes the top spot and Taiwan climbs to third. Lutnick is pushing Apple away from Chinese memory chips, the policy tailwind behind the semiconductor trade. Underneath it all, tax revenue is up 65 percent to $5.3 trillion against spending up 96 percent to $7.3 trillion, and the 30-year at 5.3 percent is the funding cost of that gap.

Fed minutes, PMIs, and the jobs and CPI prints out of Europe, Britain, Japan and Canada decide the week. Gold near $5,000 against a dollar at new lows is the trade to watch. If the long end keeps selling into data that should have helped it, that is the regime change confirming itself, not a pullback.
