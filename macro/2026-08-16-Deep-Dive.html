Iran kept the strait all week and the oil market is paying for it

The Oman deal never reopened Hormuz, Iran set six maximal preconditions, oil crossed $170 in Asia, and the odds of a normal strait this year are now 40 percent.

GeopoliticsCommoditiesRatesDollar

Every edition this channel published this week carried the same driver, and it resolved against the people who wanted a deal. Monday the strait and the route got separated out loud. Friday the blockade was indefinite and crude had gone through $170. This memo is the full arc, both cases, and the one set of prints that settles it.

The single line that explains all of it is from Monday. Mohsen Rezaei, Iran's national security secretary, said if an Iran-Oman transit agreement is reached, it will be a separate matter from the closure of the strait. Per the Market list, he set the reopening price three minutes earlier: the war ended, Iranian funds unfrozen, and other conditions passed through mediators.

That is the thing I got wrong twice, and naming it is the point of this memo. I kept treating the route as a proxy for the strait. Iran spent the entire week explaining that it is not.

1. The route is real and the strait is a different negotiation

The Oman track genuinely is alive. Qatar says the Iran-Oman negotiations are at an advanced stage with positive feedback, and Pakistan's defence minister told Bloomberg the US and Iran are close to some sort of arrangement. The sticking point is a toll. Iran wants its right to collect tolls on transit recognised, Washington disagrees, and that rejection triggered the worst fighting since the April ceasefire.

The toll dispute is small enough to sound trivial and structural enough to be the whole story. A chokepoint state asserting a toll on the world's crude is a sovereignty claim. Oman has reportedly secured Gulf Arab backing for a voluntary toll system, and the two capitals remain far apart on it. Iran absorbed the strikes without conceding, so the price of a deal went up rather than down.

Trump's framing that Iran agrees privately and refuses publicly is consistent with both a genuine negotiation and a stall. There is no way to tell them apart from outside, and this week the market stopped trying.

2. The maximal ask is what makes this a war economy, not a trade dispute

Iran's stated preconditions to reopen Hormuz are explicit and total. Per the Polymarket list: US forces withdrawn from the region, the naval blockade lifted, sanctions removed and frozen assets released, an end to proxy warfare, war reparations, and an end to hostile rhetoric. Rezaei then said Iran has no talks to extend the ceasefire, that it never officially took effect, and that Washington breached it inside 48 hours.

Read that list against the physical market and the shape of it is a war economy. Oil traded above $170 a barrel in Asia, diesel cracks broke through $98 after the Houthis hit a Saudi Aramco refinery, US gas is $4.07 a gallon, and the strategic petroleum reserve sits at its lowest since 1983 after a 52 percent drawdown in five years. The Reaper drone fleet is down about a quarter, with 45 MQ-9s lost at a replacement cost above $1.3 billion, and the Pentagon is seeking $67 billion to replenish them. Washington ordered embassies to draw up reduced-staff plans.

The pricing is the honest part. Kalshi puts only a 40 percent chance Hormuz traffic returns to normal this year, down from the near-certainty that opened the window. A separate contract on the blockade ending by year end decayed from above 95 cents to about 81. The market spent the week pricing the deal further away.

3. The bull case: this is the setup for a hard spike, and the sellers have nothing left

The escalation side is real and named. Bessent says Washington is readying an economic isolation of Iran the world has never seen, and the UAE accuses Iran of a third attack on its ADNOC facilities. Trump keeps threatening to declare Hormuz US territory. Only two vessels crossed the strait on Friday and neither carried crude. A market oversupplied on paper at 106.3 million barrels a day against 104.8 million of demand is closed at a chokepoint it cannot control, and the last time a supply shock of this shape hit, Brent blew through every $120 call.

The physical buffer is the bear's problem. The strategic reserve at 1983 lows, diesel cracks at all-time highs, and a strait carrying a fifth of global LNG closed to that traffic means there is no cushion left to absorb the next incident. One drone on a refinery moved cracks $4 to $5. The base is not a stable $170. It is a market one headline from materially higher.

4. The bear case: the reroute is real, demand is soft, and every spike has been faded

The other half is that the physical market keeps finding supply around the blockage. Iran confirmed an agreement with Oman on a new Hormuz shipping-traffic plan, the classic way a chokepoint trade unwinds. US crude stockpiles posted an unexpected build, Russian output ran nearly a million barrels a day below its OPEC quota, and US imports of Venezuelan crude hit a nine-year high. The IEA revised 2025 demand up to 104.8 million barrels a day, but that is demand growth while the shortage has not shown up.

And oil fell on the day Iran asserted control. That reaction is the case for the fade trade. The market has now twice priced a reroute and sold the escalation, and that is why crude sits at $170 instead of higher. A war premium only persists while the market believes the supply is actually trapped, and the tankers keep finding a way around.

5. The arbitration, and the prints that settle it

Both cases are live because they are describing different intervals. The bear case is a within-week fade on every headline. The bull case is a structural argument that the buffer is gone and the next incident compounds. I opened this week's Hormuz call at 70/30 and it finished at zero, resolved as a miss on Tuesday. The lesson was pricing the route as the strait. I am not repeating it.

I am 55/45 that oil is higher this time next month than it is today, and that is a low-conviction number for a reason. The difference between the Oman toll deal and the next ADNOC attack is a gap in either direction, and both are genuinely on the table.

The yen is the part this channel has been flagging all week and it is the quiet link. Japan's two-year yield hit 1.64 percent, the highest since 1995, and the yen pushed back toward 160, the level that triggered the first coordinated US-Japan intervention since 1998 and the Bessent proposal to expand the FIMA repo facility. Oil above $170 and a yen at 160 is how a war premium and a funding squeeze arrive in the same tape. If the long end keeps selling into prints that should have helped it, that is the regime change this channel called last week, not a pullback.
