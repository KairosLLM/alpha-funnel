The consumer cracked and the pricing would not budge

July retail sales fell 0.6 percent, sentiment sagged to 51, and the market still prices an 80 percent chance the Fed hikes next year.

RatesInflationGeopoliticsDollar

Yesterday the driver was the rate-hike trade breaking. Today the data joined it, and nothing moved. That gap between a weakening consumer and stubbornly hawkish pricing is the story.

Market context. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,798.99, up 0.65 percent and clearing 7,800 intraday for the second straight session, with the Nasdaq up 0.81 percent. The VIX has traded in lockstep with the index six of the last nine days and sits where a sub-13 print would be a caution flag. The US financial conditions index is near 1.29, the easiest reading since 1997, and corporate credit spreads are the tightest since 1998. Oil fell about 2 percent on Thursday, but that is the least important part of the tape.

1. The consumer cracked, and the market priced nothing. July retail sales fell 0.6 percent month over month against a 0.1 percent gain expected, the first negative print since March, and the control group fell 0.4 percent. The Per the People to follow list, University of Michigan sentiment index came in at 51 against 55 expected, and one-year inflation expectations reaccelerated to 4.3 percent from 4.2. The soft read landed on top of a cooler PPI, unchanged month over month and up 4.7 percent year over year, which dialed back September hike odds further.

None of it moved the pricing that matters. Kalshi still puts an 80 percent chance on a Fed rate hike next year. The thirty-year sits higher than it did before the prints. Weak data, cold inflation, and a bond market that will not take the bait is the needle Bessent and Warsh are trying to thread, and today it held in their favor. The consumer is the lag, not the lead, and this market keeps pricing the Fed's inflation worry over the consumer's.

2. The blockade is now indefinite, and the strait never returned to normal. The United States said it could maintain the naval blockade of Iran indefinitely. Kalshi puts only a 40 percent chance that Hormuz traffic returns to normal this year, down from the near-certainty that opened the window. A tanker was hit by a drone in the strait, and the first Kharg VLCC loaded 2 million barrels, the first such loading in two weeks. The Defense Department signed framework agreements with Boeing and Raytheon to raise SM-3 interceptor component production, a quiet signal that Washington is planning for a long standoff, not a quick one.

This is the same thread the house flagged when the July 27 Abqaiq strike was first reported. Oil is down on the day because the physical market has priced the reroute, but the chokepoint risk is structural and the odds have not improved.

3. The AI financing loop is the risk the record hides. Michael Burry's circular-financing map has about $46 billion of direct equity cycling against $879 billion of purchase commitments across Oracle, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, CoreWeave, Nvidia and AMD, and the Bank for International Settlements counts $1.65 trillion of hyperscaler off-balance-sheet obligations against $1.35 trillion reported. Jack Mallers called OpenAI's $250 billion Ohio build-out outrageous, arguing it is 135 percent of the company's retained earnings and that a 100-times-cheaper Chinese alternative would break the creditworthiness the whole stack leans on.

The bull case and the bear case agree on the mechanism, and that is why it is worth stating plainly. The record is being funded by off-balance-sheet commitments that no earnings stream yet services. The market is pricing the build, not the financing, and the financing is the part that can turn.

4. The dollar is cracking against Asia while the deficit sets records. The Chinese yuan is at its strongest level against the dollar since 2023, and $131 billion flowed into Japanese stocks with the Nikkei up 1.75 percent as the BOJ, Japan's central bank, moves closer to a hike as soon as September. The July deficit printed at a record $432 billion, the national debt sits at a record $39.9 trillion, and Fitch affirmed the United States at AA+ with a stable outlook. The dollar is doing the opposite of what the fiscal numbers imply, and the yen's drift back toward the intervention zone keeps the funding question alive.
