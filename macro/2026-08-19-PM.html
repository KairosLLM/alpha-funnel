The Treasury stepped in and the long end broke the other way

Buybacks double to $4 billion an operation and the 30-year falls below 5.2 percent, gold jumps 3.5 percent, and the dollar hits a three-month low.

RatesInflationDollarCommoditiesLiquidity

Yesterday the question was whether the long end could keep selling into data that should have helped it. Today the Treasury answered, and it answered by stepping in: buybacks on 10- to 30-year debt double to $4 billion a run starting September 9, the 30-year broke below 5.20 percent, and the debasement hedges that broke yesterday came roaring back. The minutes land this afternoon as the second act.

Market context. The buyback announcement was the whole tape. Per the People list, the 30-year fell 8 basis points, gold rose 3.5 percent, and the dollar dropped 0.7 percent, with Bitcoin ripping higher on a record short squeeze. Per the Polymarket list, the 30-year fell below 5.20 percent, and the dollar hit its lowest level in over three months. Copper sits at a record near $6.77 a pound. Per the Financial Markets list, crude stockpiles rose 4.4 million barrels to 428.8 million, a third straight build and the highest since May, even as Iran tensions stay hot. The Fed minutes land at 2pm. This is a trading weekday, so the recap is live.

The scoreboard. The call that anchored this week, that the 30-year at 5.3 percent would decide between regime change and a pullback, got its first real answer today and it was the pullback side: the Treasury doubled the buybacks and the long end caught a bid before the minutes even printed. The oil call is still live into Friday, with the crude build and the Hormuz blockade both unresolved.

1. The Treasury decided the long end would not run and the whole trade flipped. The mechanism is direct. Per the People list, the Treasury is at least doubling the size of its liquidity-support buybacks on longer-dated debt, to $4 billion per operation from $2 billion, effective September 9. That is the government buying the bond it had been flooding, and the market read it exactly as the hedge reversal: the 30-year fell 8 basis points and gold, which broke with the long end yesterday, jumped 3.5 percent.

The read is split on what it means. Per the Polymarket list, one camp calls it liquidity support, not QE, the argument being it eases the back of the curve rather than expanding the balance sheet. The other calls it the first move of fiscal dominance, with Per the Kalshi list, gold adding $3.5 trillion to its market cap in 21 days, up 12.5 percent. Either way, the line the house has watched all week, the long end as the driver that breaks the hedges, has now been answered by policy.

2. The Fed minutes land at 2pm and they are the second act. The buyback took the long end down before the minutes could, but the minutes are still the test of whether this is regime change or a managed pullback. The market had already priced the world they have to confirm or deny: the 30-year is back under 5.20 percent, and the question hanging over the book is whether Warsh can hike into a Treasury that is now buying its own long end.

Per Into the Cryptoverse, Benjamin Cowen still reads the rate-cutting cycle as over, with a hike before year-end and the Fed holding at 4 percent through 2027, and he makes the contrarian call that the dollar rallies near term toward 105 to 106 before the next leg down. That is the direct counter to the dollar trade today, and the minutes are the arbiter.

3. The dollar broke to a three-month low and the yen carry is the next shoe. The DXY drop is the tell on how far the buyback news reached: Per the People list, the dollar fell 0.7 percent to its lowest in more than three months on the same day the Treasury moved. The bearish dollar call now has a policy catalyst behind it, but the setup underneath is unchanged.

Per the overnight trading video, Japan's 30-year surged above 4 percent and the BOJ's intervention level sits at 163, the next test if 160 breaks. The carry trade borrows from Japan near 1 percent to buy US long end near 5 percent, and that spread is what keeps the unwind risk live. A Treasury that bids its own long end makes the carry more attractive, not less, and that is the next shoe on this desk.

4. Oil holds Hormuz and the swing is still two-way. The crude build is a real easing signal on supply. Per the Financial Markets list, crude stockpiles rose 4.4 million barrels to 428.8 million, a third straight increase and the highest since May, and that is the de-escalation input that helps the long end. It is not breaking the war risk. Trump has halted the Iran talks and moved to the strangle posture, the UAE has suspended trade with Iran, and the blockade stands with Hormuz open and its mines removed. Per the Polymarket list, Hormuz traffic returning to normal by the end of the year sits at 37 percent, so the market still prices a meaningful chance of a deal.

That is the same two-way open the house flagged Friday, and it is still open: the build helps the long end while a fresh strike would push oil toward the $100 forecasts and confirm the regime.

5. What to watch: the minutes, then whether gold holds. The buyback is the strongest signal yet that the long end is a policy fight, not a free fall, and gold holding its 3.5 percent gain through the minutes would confirm the hedge trade is back. Cowen's dollar-higher read is the contrarian test on the other side. The minutes answer the rates half, the next oil print answers the commodity half, and the two together decide whether today was the regime reversal or just the first buyback bounce.

Fed minutes at 2pm. The long end against gold is the trade this book has been watching all week.
