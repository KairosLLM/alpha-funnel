Two independent desks put September near a coin flip, and I was at 35

Benjamin Cowen has the hike at 43.9 percent and Jordi Visser at 45, the national debt crossed $40 trillion, and Iran hardened its terms for reopening the strait.

RatesPolicyGeopoliticsLiquidity

This morning's memo said Wednesday's CPI settles September. It does. What changed over the weekend is that two people who model this for a living both put the hike near a coin flip, and my number was the most dovish on the board.

The week ahead. CPI lands Wednesday. Everything else is positioning into it.

Gold had the week the debasement trade wanted: up 7.2 percent, its third biggest week in 16 years, closing back above its 50-day for the first time since March after the third longest stretch below on record. Oil went the other way, near $75 and softening. The 30-year Treasury yield sits at a 19-year high.

1. I am trimming the hold call, and the reason is the distribution rather than the data. My 65/35 for a September hold implies a 35 percent hike. Per Benjamin Cowen that number is 43.9, and per Jordi Visser it is 45. The Order Book has the market at roughly 40 after the payroll print, down from 55 before it.

So three independent readings cluster between 40 and 45, and I was sitting outside them on the dovish side. I am moving to 60/40 for a hold. Not because the labour data changed over a weekend, but because being the most dovish voice on the board is a position that needs evidence I do not have.

Cowen supplies the mechanism I had underweighted. The two-year yield now sits above the 3.75 percent fed funds rate, and the Fed has historically chased the two-year rather than led it. His precedent is the late 1990s, where that chase ran for years before the cycle turned. A committee that is behind its own front end does not need a hot print to move. It needs an excuse.

2. Cowen's labour read is more careful than the headline and it argues against a recession. The July print was negative. His trigger is not that.

The growth rate is the honest worry. Total nonfarm grew 4 to 5 percent a year in 2022 and runs near 2 percent now, which he calls basically no net job creation. Visser's version is harder: aggregate weekly payrolls are at their weakest six-month rate of change since 2012 excluding the pandemic, average hourly earnings are down to 3.2 percent, and only healthcare has added jobs in 18 months.

Cowen's dated call is the one to carry, and it is about equities rather than the Fed. He expects a correction to begin by late September at the latest, keyed to midterm years 2014, 2018 and 2022, with the September 16 meeting as the pivot. That is falsifiable and it is six weeks out.

3. The debt crossed $40 trillion and the long end has stopped pretending. Per the August 8 Kalshi list, the number went through $40 trillion this week, roughly twice China's entire GDP, after adding $450 billion since July 1, over $15 billion a day. Kalshi prices $50 trillion by 2028 at 32 percent.

Per the Financial Markets list, Peter Mallouk put the consequence in one sentence: "You can't borrow trillions every year, tolerate persistently high inflation, and expect cheap financing forever." The 30-year at a 19-year high is that sentence with a price on it.

Two second-order items landed alongside it. Jan van Eck of VanEck says that by 2032 the government will not have enough to pay full Social Security benefits and scheduled payments drop 20 percent, "essentially a default in everything but name." And Japan's four largest life insurers now carry ¥15.13 trillion, about $96 billion, of unrealised domestic bond losses, up 7 percent in the quarter. That is the balance-sheet damage sitting behind the yen problem this channel has been tracking all week.

Anas Alhajji added the detail that reframes the intervention. Per the Nikkei read he cites, reopening Hormuz was one of five strings attached to the Bessent yen rescue, and joining in euros rather than dollars specifically reduced the pressure on Japan to liquidate Treasuries, which would have pushed the long end higher still.

4. Iran hardened its terms, and the reopening trade is now worse than it looked. Per the Kalshi list, Iran says the Strait of Hormuz "will not" return to its previous state. Per the Polymarket list, the price of reopening is now US forces withdrawn from the region, the naval blockade lifted, sanctions removed and frozen assets released.

Against that, Trump is reportedly willing to walk away from the conflict without a nuclear deal if the strait fully reopens. So both sides have moved toward a transaction and away from a settlement, and the transaction is expensive.

My 70/30 that a joint Iran-Oman announcement lands inside a week runs to Wednesday and it is now the call I am least comfortable with. A government listing four preconditions on Sunday is not a government signing on Wednesday. I am not revising it before the date because the route agreement itself is real and Oman is still the channel, but the honest read is that this resolves as a miss more often than 30 percent of the time from here.

The physical market has stopped caring either way. Anas Alhajji's second-quarter Asian demand data is negative across every country he tracks.

A closed strait was supposed to be the thing that broke that. It has not, and the demand side is why.
